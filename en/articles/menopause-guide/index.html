Menopause: What Every Woman Needs to Know About This Stage

By Adnan Alrefai · 14 July 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Menopause is a natural stage usually beginning between the ages of 45 and 55, diagnosed after twelve consecutive months without a period.
  • The most common symptoms are hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disturbance and mood changes. A 2013 Saudi study confirmed these are just as prevalent in Arab women as in Western populations.
  • The most significant long-term effect is accelerated bone loss, raising fracture risk, alongside cardiovascular changes that deserve attention.
  • Hormone therapy relieves symptoms and protects bones for many women, but it is not right for everyone and requires honest discussion with your doctor.
  • There is no need to suffer in silence. Medical options and lifestyle changes make a real difference to quality of life in these years.

What Is Menopause and How Is It Diagnosed?

Menopause is the natural stage of a woman's life when the ovaries stop releasing eggs and levels of the female hormones estrogen and progesterone fall. It is diagnosed after twelve consecutive months with no period and no other explanation such as pregnancy or illness.

This is preceded by a transitional period that can last two to eight years, called perimenopause. During perimenopause periods become irregular, lengthening or shortening unpredictably, and some symptoms may already begin. Many women mistake this phase for a hormonal disorder that needs urgent treatment, when in fact it is a normal transition.

Natural menopause usually occurs between 45 and 55, with an average around 51. Early menopause before age 40 deserves specific medical evaluation and may result from genetic causes, autoimmune conditions, chemotherapy or radiotherapy. It carries higher long-term risks for bones and the heart and needs a tailored plan.

Culturally in the Arab region, menopause is often surrounded by silence or by language that amplifies a woman's fears rather than helping her understand what is happening. A 2013 study published in the journal Climacteric exploring the experience of menopause among Arab women in Qatar found that many described this stage with pessimism and resignation, a pattern the researchers linked to insufficient accurate information rather than to the experience itself. Open conversation with a female doctor or with a trusted woman who has passed through this stage dissolves much of the anxiety that information could prevent.

What Symptoms Are Most Common in Arab Women?

A 2013 study published in the Annals of Saudi Medicine involving women in Riyadh found that hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes and insomnia were among the most frequently reported symptoms, consistent with what is seen globally. A 2021 UAE study found that women's awareness of menopause symptoms and treatment options remained limited, meaning many women endure symptoms that can be relieved.

Physical symptoms include hot flashes, which are sudden intense heat rising from the chest to the face lasting minutes, night sweats that disrupt sleep, vaginal dryness that can cause discomfort during intercourse, and more frequent urination with occasional difficulty controlling the bladder. Emotional symptoms include mood swings, difficulty concentrating and a sense of irritability that can be hard to separate from the life pressures many women carry at this stage.

A 2019 study of Emirati women published in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health found that menopause-specific quality of life was significantly affected in the vasomotor and sexual symptom dimensions. This confirms that seeking medical support at this stage is a well-documented need, not a sign of fragility.

Some symptoms deserve separate evaluation because they may have a different cause than menopause: vaginal bleeding that returns after twelve months of silence, sudden severe headache, or strong palpitations. These should not be automatically attributed to menopause and need a doctor's assessment.

Symptoms to Never Ignore: See Your Doctor

  • Vaginal bleeding after twelve months without a period
  • Rapid or irregular heartbeat that worries you
  • A sudden severe headache unlike any you have had before
  • Chest pressure, pain or shortness of breath
  • Severe dizziness or fainting

What Does Menopause Do to Bone Health?

Estrogen protects bone density, and as it falls at menopause, bone loss accelerates sharply, especially in the first five years after the final period. A 2021 review in Best Practice and Research in Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism confirms that hormone therapy started in this window meaningfully slows bone loss.

Osteoporosis means reduced bone density and a higher risk of fracture from a minor impact. Hip and spine fractures caused by post-menopausal osteoporosis can severely affect a woman's independence and mobility. Some hip fractures in older women carry serious complications in the first year after the break, which underscores why prevention in the perimenopausal window matters so much. Prevention starts before the bone loss becomes established, not after a fracture occurs.

A bone density scan (DEXA scan) is recommended for every woman after menopause, especially if she is slim-framed, a smoker, has a family history of hip fractures, or has taken corticosteroids for extended periods. The result gives a number called a T-score that your doctor will interpret and use to plan prevention or treatment. A T-score below minus 2.5 means established osteoporosis and warrants treatment; a score between minus 1 and minus 2.5 means osteopenia, a state of reduced but not yet critically low bone density where prevention remains highly effective.

What Changes for Heart Health?

Before menopause, estrogen protects the blood vessel walls, reduces inflammation and slows arterial stiffening. After menopause, LDL cholesterol tends to rise, arterial flexibility declines and cardiovascular risk gradually increases, approaching the level seen in men by the late seventies.

This does not mean menopause causes heart disease directly. It means the natural hormonal protection diminishes, which makes attention to blood pressure, cholesterol, physical activity and body weight more important than ever in this stage.

Women who experience early menopause before age 45 carry a higher future cardiovascular risk, which is one reason these cases need careful assessment. Annual monitoring of blood pressure, lipids and blood sugar becomes a worthwhile investment in health from this stage onwards.

Hormone Therapy: Who Benefits and Who Should Not Take It?

Hormone therapy means replacing the estrogen the ovaries have stopped producing, sometimes combined with progesterone. A 2024 review in JAMA that analysed decades of Women's Health Initiative data concluded that hormone therapy effectively relieves hot flashes, sleep disturbance and vaginal dryness, reduces bone loss, and that timing matters: starting within the first ten years of menopause and before age 60 gives the best benefit-to-risk ratio.

Women who still have a uterus need progesterone alongside estrogen to protect the uterine lining. Women who have had a hysterectomy can take estrogen alone. The treatment comes in tablets, skin patches, gels and vaginal rings, and your doctor has a role in choosing the most suitable form for your situation.

Hormone therapy is not suitable for women who have had breast cancer or uterine cancer, those with a history of deep vein thrombosis or stroke, or those with active coronary artery disease. It also needs careful evaluation where significant breast cancer risk factors are present. A frank conversation with your doctor about your personal risk profile is the essential first step.

Who Should Not Start Hormone Therapy

  • Past history of breast cancer or cancer of the uterine lining
  • Previous deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism
  • A prior stroke or heart attack
  • Unexplained vaginal bleeding
  • Severe active liver disease or cirrhosis

Alternatives for Women Who Cannot Take Hormone Therapy

For women who are not candidates for hormone therapy or prefer to avoid it, there are effective alternatives. Certain antidepressants in the SSRI and SNRI family, such as venlafaxine, reduce hot flashes by a meaningful margin and are particularly useful for women who also experience mood changes. Gabapentin can help with nocturnal hot flashes in some women. Newer non-hormonal medications have also been approved in some countries specifically for moderate to severe hot flashes, so the landscape of options is broader than it was a decade ago.

For vaginal dryness specifically, local vaginal estrogen creams work only at the site and have negligible systemic absorption, making them acceptable for most women including many who have restrictions on systemic estrogen. This distinction between treating systemic symptoms and treating local ones is important and often overlooked. Vaginal dryness and the discomfort it causes are addressable, and a woman should not feel she has to simply accept them.

Lifestyle adjustments produce real results: keeping the bedroom cool at night, wearing light layers, avoiding common triggers like caffeine, hot drinks and acute stress, and exercising regularly to improve sleep and mood. These are not soft suggestions but strategies with evidence behind them. In settings where discussing symptoms openly is difficult, keeping a private symptom log in a health app can help you arrive at a medical appointment with precise information that makes the conversation shorter and more effective.

Staying Healthy Through and After Menopause

Calcium and vitamin D are the foundations of bone protection at this stage. Good dietary sources of calcium include dairy, sesame seeds, almonds, dark leafy vegetables and pulses. Vitamin D deficiency is common across the Arab region even with abundant sunshine, due to clothing patterns and limited direct sun exposure, so testing and supplementation guided by your doctor is often warranted.

Regular physical activity is doubly valuable: weight-bearing exercise such as walking, stair climbing and light resistance training stimulates bone-building cells and slows bone loss, while cardiovascular exercise reduces heart risk. Thirty minutes of walking on most days of the week is a practical and cost-free starting point, and studies of women in midlife consistently confirm that those who exercise regularly report fewer severe menopausal symptoms and better sleep quality than sedentary peers.

Healthy fats, adequate protein, and plenty of vegetables and fruit are the nutritional cornerstones of this stage. The decline in estrogen shifts fat distribution toward the abdomen even in women whose total weight changes little, and this abdominal fat pattern raises the risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes, making diet quality more consequential than at any earlier stage. Reducing added sugar, excess salt and saturated fat are specific and manageable targets that each carry meaningful long-term benefit.

Calcium and vitamin D deserve a separate mention. Aim for around 1,200 mg of calcium per day from food sources first: dairy, sesame, almonds, dark leafy greens and pulses are all good sources within regional diets. Vitamin D deficiency is common across the Arab world even with abundant sunshine, because clothing patterns and limited direct skin exposure reduce synthesis. Testing your vitamin D level and supplementing under your doctor's guidance is often warranted and is a simple, low-cost measure.

When Should You See a Doctor?

Menopause is not a condition that requires a doctor only in a crisis. Women benefit from regular contact with a doctor or gynaecologist through this transition. The first appointment to discuss symptoms and options should not be delayed if your symptoms are affecting your sleep, your work, your relationship or your daily quality of life.

Vaginal bleeding that returns after twelve months of silence is not a normal part of menopause and needs prompt medical evaluation, because its causes include uterine lining changes and uterine cancer that can be detected early when investigated quickly. This is not a situation to wait and see.

Frequently asked questions

Is early menopause before age 40 dangerous?

Early menopause, also called premature ovarian insufficiency, carries a higher long-term risk of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease than natural menopause, because the woman loses the protection of estrogen for more years. It warrants thorough medical evaluation and discussion of protective options tailored to your situation.

Do hot flashes always get worse over time?

In most women, hot flashes peak in the first one to two years and then gradually decrease, disappearing within three to seven years for many. Some women experience them for longer. Their continuation does not indicate a serious problem, but if they affect your sleep and life, effective treatment options exist.

Can hormone therapy increase the risk of breast cancer?

This deserves a straight answer. The 2024 JAMA review of the Women's Health Initiative indicates that combined estrogen-plus-progesterone therapy for five years or more is associated with a small increase in breast cancer risk, while estrogen alone in women without a uterus does not show this increase. Your individual risk depends on your personal history and other factors and should be discussed with your doctor.

What happens to sexual desire at menopause?

Many women experience reduced sexual desire, often linked to vaginal dryness and the discomfort it causes, alongside mood changes. This is common and treatable. Local vaginal moisturisers and topical estrogen improve dryness, and open communication with a partner and support from a counsellor can also help. There is no awkwardness in raising this with your doctor as it directly affects quality of life.

Are herbal supplements like red clover safe for symptoms?

Some herbal products including soy phytoestrogens and red clover are marketed for hot flash relief. Evidence for their effectiveness is weak and inconsistent, and some contain compounds that mimic estrogen, raising concern for women who have restrictions on estrogen. Speak with your doctor before taking any supplement.

Will I definitely gain weight after menopause?

Menopause itself does not directly cause weight gain, but hormonal changes shift fat distribution toward the abdomen even when total weight changes little. Muscle mass also tends to decline with age. Good nutrition and regular activity substantially reduce this effect.

How long does menopause last?

Menopause itself is a single point in time, diagnosed after twelve months without a period. What follows is postmenopause, which lasts the rest of life. Hot flashes gradually subside for most women within seven years, while the effects on bones and the heart require ongoing monitoring and preventive attention indefinitely.
