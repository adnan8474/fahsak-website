Dates: what the evidence actually says about their benefits and limits

By Adnan Alrefai · 14 July 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Dates contain around 8 grams of fibre and over 450 mg of potassium per 100 g, both well documented in peer-reviewed research.
  • Every 100 g of dried dates also delivers around 75 g of carbohydrates, almost all as sugars, so portion size matters enormously.
  • A 2016 Saudi study measuring 17 date varieties found most fall in the low to moderate glycaemic index range, but glycaemic load rises sharply with large portions.
  • A 2020 randomised controlled trial in Bahrain found that small daily portions did not raise blood glucose in people with pre-diabetes or type 2 diabetes.
  • If you have diabetes, measure your blood glucose two hours after eating dates to know your personal response rather than relying on general tables.

What do dates actually contain?

Dates (Phoenix dactylifera) have been grown across the Arab world for thousands of years, and their cultural status has generated more confident nutrition claims than the evidence can always support. The truth is more nuanced than either the enthusiastic promotion or the blanket warnings suggest.

A comprehensive review published in Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition in 2008 by Al-Farsi and Lee documented the nutritional composition of dates in detail. Per 100 g of dried dates: roughly 314 kcal, approximately 75 g of carbohydrates mostly as fructose and glucose, and around 8 g of dietary fibre. That fibre content is genuinely significant because it slows glucose absorption and supports digestive health.

The potassium content is also notable. Some varieties studied in Oman exceeded 450 mg per 100 g, which is a meaningful contribution toward the daily requirement for heart health and blood pressure regulation. Dates also carry magnesium, manganese, and antioxidant compounds including flavonoids, carotenoids and phenolic acids.

The honest summary: dates are not empty calories, and they are not a superfood cure. They are a nutrient-dense fruit whose only genuine complexity is that the nutrient density extends to sugar as well as micronutrients.

Is the glycaemic index of dates really low?

This is the question every practitioner in the region hears regularly, and it deserves a precise answer rather than a reassuring one. In 2016, Saudi researchers measured the glycaemic index (GI) of seventeen date varieties grown in the Kingdom and published their findings in the Annals of Saudi Medicine. Most varieties tested fell in the low to moderate GI range, roughly between 35 and 55, which is better than many people expect given how sweet dates taste.

However, glycaemic index on its own is an incomplete picture. Glycaemic load (GL) combines GI with the actual amount eaten, and this is where dates become more nuanced. Two or three dates carry a modest glycaemic load. Ten or fifteen dates, which is not unusual during Ramadan evening gatherings, produce a glycaemic load that can noticeably raise blood glucose even in people without diabetes.

The practical takeaway: the moderate GI of dates means glucose rises more slowly than with refined sugar, not that quantity does not matter. Quantity matters a great deal, and this distinction is consistently lost in popular writing about dates.

What clinical trials say about dates and blood glucose

The most directly relevant clinical evidence comes from a randomised controlled trial published in Nutrients in 2020. Researchers in Bahrain recruited adults with pre-diabetes or type 2 diabetes and gave one group small daily portions of dates over three months while the control group did not eat them. The date group showed no deterioration in glycaemic control and had some improvements in lipid profile markers compared to the control group. This is a meaningful finding because it challenges the reflexive advice to avoid all sweet fruits in diabetes management.

This finding matters, but it comes with important context. The portions were small and carefully controlled, the trial ran for three months under supervision, and the participants were not eating unlimited dates. You cannot extrapolate these results to unrestricted quantities or to every individual regardless of their medication, current blood glucose control, or kidney function.

If you have diabetes and want to include dates in your diet, the most reliable approach is to check your glucose before eating dates and two hours afterwards on several occasions. Individual glycaemic responses vary far more than population-level tables suggest, and your personal readings tell you more than any general guideline. Some people with well-controlled type 2 diabetes find that two or three dates produce a modest, acceptable rise; others see a significant spike that warrants keeping portions smaller or less frequent.

Health benefits that are genuinely supported

Setting aside the glycaemic debate, several benefits from dates are well supported. The 8 g of dietary fibre per 100 g supports bowel regularity and feeds the intestinal microbiome. Higher fibre intake is consistently associated in large studies with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease and colorectal cancer, and dates contribute meaningfully to daily fibre targets.

The high potassium content is a legitimate cardiovascular benefit. Potassium helps counterbalance the effect of sodium on blood pressure, and most people in the Arab world eat more sodium than recommended and less potassium than optimal. Dates, eaten in reasonable quantities, help shift that balance in the right direction.

The antioxidant compounds in dates, including flavonoids, carotenoids and phenolic acids, are documented in laboratory analyses. Whether these translate into meaningful disease prevention in humans at the quantities realistically eaten requires more clinical evidence, but they represent real chemistry rather than marketing language.

Dates in Ramadan: how to benefit without overdoing it

Breaking the fast with two or three dates and water has a sound nutritional rationale. After hours without food, the natural sugars in dates raise blood glucose at a pace moderated by fibre, while the potassium and magnesium contribute to rehydration. This traditional practice aligns well with what nutritional science would recommend for a gentle fast-breaking.

The challenge comes later in the evening when dates are passed around freely and people often eat ten, fifteen or more without noticing. At these quantities, sugars accumulate rapidly and the fibre benefit that moderates blood glucose is overwhelmed. For someone with diabetes or someone trying to manage their weight, this is the part of Ramadan that deserves careful attention, not the two or three dates at the call to prayer.

A practical approach: break your fast with two or three dates and water as the traditional practice recommends, then move to your main meal. If you want more dates later in the evening during socialising, portion them out deliberately and count them. Each medium-sized date contains roughly 20 to 25 kcal and about 5 g of sugar, which means ten dates in an evening sitting provides 50 g of sugar from that single source alone.

For people with diabetes who fast during Ramadan, endocrinologists across the region recommend checking blood glucose before breaking the fast and two hours afterwards. If your glucose is already low before iftar, two or three dates provide a reasonable and measured way to bring it up gently before eating a full meal.

Common claims about dates that need correcting

Dates are sometimes promoted as a treatment for cancer, heart disease or infertility. These claims go far beyond what published evidence supports. Some laboratory studies have shown promising effects of date extracts on cancer cell lines in vitro, but what happens in a test tube is a different matter from what happens in a living person at realistic dietary intake levels.

Another common claim is that dates convert to a different kind of sugar in the body and do not raise blood glucose at all. This is simply incorrect. Dates raise blood glucose. The fibre and moderate GI mean they raise it more slowly than refined sugar, but the effect is real and measurable. Slower is not zero.

A third overstatement is that dates strengthen bones because of their mineral content. The magnesium and manganese are real, but the quantities are modest and no clinical trial has demonstrated that eating dates protects against osteoporosis. A food that contains a mineral is not a treatment for deficiency of that mineral.

Popular claims about dates that lack sufficient evidence

  • Dates treat or prevent cancer: no adequate clinical evidence in humans exists
  • Dates do not raise blood glucose at all: they do, at a slower pace than refined sugar
  • Eating 10+ dates daily is beneficial for everyone: excess adds significant sugars and calories

Who should be more careful with dates?

People with diabetes who use insulin or medications that stimulate insulin release need to track date portions carefully, because the sugars add up quickly and can disrupt glycaemic control. Self-monitoring with a glucose meter before and two hours after eating dates gives an honest reading of your personal response.

People following a low-carbohydrate diet for weight loss will find that even a modest handful of dates consumes a large part of their daily carbohydrate allowance. Dates are not forbidden on such diets, but they need to be counted deliberately rather than treated as a free food.

People with chronic kidney disease who have elevated blood potassium (Hyperkalemia) should ask their doctor before increasing their date intake, because the high potassium content in dates can worsen that condition. This is not a common concern for healthy individuals but is worth flagging.

How to choose and store dates

Many commercially packaged date products in supermarkets contain added sugar or glucose syrup to extend shelf life or enhance flavour. Reading the ingredients list takes seconds and tells you immediately whether what you are buying is simply dates or a confectionery product dressed as a health food. Dates should be the only or first ingredient.

Fresh dates and naturally dried dates without additives are both good choices. Dates coated in chocolate, filled with butter, or with added sugar are essentially sweets and should be treated as such in any nutritional calculation.

Dates keep well in a sealed container away from heat and moisture. Freezing dates preserves their nutritional quality for months, which is useful if you buy them in seasonal bulk during harvest periods.

Where dates fit in a healthy diet

Dates belong in a balanced diet as a nutrient-dense fruit eaten in reasonable portions, not as a supplement, not as a medicine, and not as an unlimited snack. For most healthy adults, three to five dates daily as part of a varied diet adds genuine nutritional value without a meaningful downside.

The evidence from a randomised controlled trial suggests that even for people with pre-diabetes or type 2 diabetes, small controlled portions do not necessarily worsen glycaemic control. But this is not a licence to eat freely: it means careful, measured inclusion with glucose monitoring is likely safe for many people with diabetes, not that the concern was misplaced.

What this page should leave you with: dates are genuinely nutritious and have a real place in regional diets. The exaggerated claims in both directions, that they cure everything or must be avoided by anyone with a health concern, serve no one. Informed moderation, based on knowing what you are eating and how your body responds, is the only approach that works.

Frequently asked questions

How many dates can I eat per day?

For a healthy adult without diabetes, three to five dates daily as part of a balanced diet is a reasonable amount. If you are monitoring your weight or managing diabetes, discuss the right portion with your doctor and measure your glucose two hours after eating them to understand your personal response.

Do dates raise blood sugar?

Yes, but more slowly than refined sugar because of their fibre content and moderate glycaemic index. A 2016 study of 17 Saudi date varieties found most fall in the low to moderate GI range. Glycaemic load still rises significantly with large portions, so quantity matters as much as the GI figure.

Are dates good for blood pressure?

Dates are a meaningful source of potassium, which supports heart health and helps counteract the effect of dietary sodium on blood pressure. This is a documented nutritional benefit, not marketing. However, dates alone cannot treat high blood pressure and should not replace prescribed medication.

Can people with diabetes eat dates?

A 2020 randomised controlled trial found that small daily portions did not worsen glycaemic control in people with pre-diabetes or type 2 diabetes and may have improved some lipid markers. This does not mean unlimited consumption is safe. Small, counted portions with glucose monitoring is the approach backed by evidence.

Do dates cause weight gain?

Dates are calorie-dense, with around 314 kcal per 100 g, and it is easy to eat a larger quantity than you realise. Two or three dates have a modest caloric impact; eating fifteen or more in an evening adds up significantly. Weight change depends on total caloric intake, not any single food.

Are Medjool dates healthier than other varieties?

Medjool dates are larger and have a soft, caramel-like texture, but nutritionally they are broadly comparable to other premium varieties. The 2016 Saudi study found variation in GI and nutrient content across 17 varieties, but no single variety was dramatically superior. Choosing any high-quality natural date without added sugar is the most important factor.

Is it true dates have no added sugar?

Natural dates contain only their own sugars. However, many commercially packaged date products do contain added sugar, glucose syrup or other sweeteners to extend shelf life. Always read the ingredients list and choose products where dates are the only listed ingredient.

Are dates safe to eat during Ramadan if I have diabetes?

Breaking the fast with two or three dates has a reasonable nutritional rationale and aligns with what research on dates and blood glucose suggests is safe in small portions. The risk in Ramadan comes from eating large quantities throughout the evening. Monitor your glucose and discuss your plan with your doctor before Ramadan begins.
