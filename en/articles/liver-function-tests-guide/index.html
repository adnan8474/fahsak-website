What Does the Liver Do and Why Does It Matter?

The liver is one of the largest and busiest organs in the body, sitting in the upper right side of the abdomen under the ribs. It acts as both a factory and a filtering station, turning food into energy, storing vitamins and sugar, and regulating the levels of many substances in the blood.

Among its most important jobs are clearing toxins and medicines from the blood, making essential proteins such as albumin and blood clotting factors, and producing bile that helps digest fats. When the liver is working well we do not feel it, which is why it is sometimes called the silent organ.

Because the liver carries out all these vital tasks, any problem with it can affect the whole body. Fortunately, the liver has a strong ability to repair itself if we address the cause early and remove what is harming it.

What Do These Enzymes and Markers in Your Test Mean?

Liver function tests are not a single test, but a group of measurements that give the doctor a picture of the liver's condition. Some of them measure enzymes that leak into the blood when liver cells are damaged, and some measure how well the liver is performing its jobs.

It helps to get to know the most common of these measurements in simple terms, while remembering that the numbers alone are not enough. The doctor is the one who reads them together and interprets them in the context of your full situation.

  • ALT (alanine transaminase): an enzyme found in large amounts inside liver cells, and its rise is one of the most telling signs of liver cell damage.
  • AST (aspartate transaminase): an enzyme found in the liver and also in muscles and the heart, so it is read alongside ALT and not on its own.
  • ALP (alkaline phosphatase): often rises in problems of the bile ducts, and may also rise for reasons related to bone.
  • Bilirubin: a yellow substance produced by the breakdown of red blood cells, and its rise can cause jaundice, meaning yellowing of the skin and eyes.
  • Albumin: a protein made by the liver, and a lasting drop in it may point to weakness in long-term liver function.

Why Do Liver Enzymes Rise?

Raised liver enzymes are not a disease in themselves, but a signal that something is upsetting the liver. The causes are many, some of them simple and temporary, and some needing follow-up and treatment.

Among the most common causes is a buildup of fat in the liver known as fatty liver, which is often linked to excess weight or raised blood sugar or blood fats. Other important causes include viral hepatitis such as hepatitis B and C, drinking alcohol, and certain medicines and supplements.

  • Fatty liver: one of the most common causes of a mild rise, linked to weight, sugar, and blood fats.
  • Viral hepatitis: such as hepatitis B and C viruses, which can remain silent for years.
  • Alcohol: repeated drinking strains the liver and raises the enzymes.
  • Certain medicines and supplements: even some common medicines or herbal supplements can raise the enzymes.

Mild Rises Are Common, and Problems Are Often Silent

It is reassuring that many cases of raised liver enzymes are mild, and may be discovered by chance during a routine test without the person feeling any symptoms. In many cases the cause is fatty liver, which improves with changes to lifestyle.

But the other side of this fact is that liver diseases are often silent in their early stages. The liver may keep working despite growing damage, with no clear pain, and this makes regular checks important, especially for those with risk factors.

For this reason, a raised result should not be ignored even if you feel completely well. It is better to show the result to your doctor, who can decide whether it needs to be repeated, followed up, or investigated further.

Jaundice and Warning Signs

Jaundice means yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes, and it happens when bilirubin rises in the blood. It is one of the clearest warning signs that calls for prompt medical review, because it may point to a problem in the liver or the bile ducts.

Alongside jaundice, there are other symptoms worth watching for, especially if they appear together or persist. Listening to your body and seeing a doctor early is far better than waiting.

  • Yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes, dark urine, or pale stools.
  • Pain or swelling in the upper right side of the abdomen.
  • Severe and unexplained tiredness, or loss of appetite and weight loss.
  • Persistent itching of the skin, or easy bruising and bleeding.

How Do You Protect Your Liver?

The good news is that much of what harms the liver can be avoided or reduced. Simple lifestyle steps can make a big difference, especially in cases of fatty liver, which improves with a healthy weight and a balanced diet.

It is also important to be cautious about hidden sources of harm, such as overusing painkillers without need, or taking unreliable herbal supplements in the belief that they help the liver. When in doubt, ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting any medicine or supplement.

  • Keep a healthy weight, cut down on sugars and processed fats, and eat more vegetables and whole grains.
  • Reduce or fully avoid alcohol, as it is one of the leading causes of liver harm.
  • Ask your doctor about testing and vaccination for hepatitis B, and about testing for hepatitis C if you have risk factors.
  • Do not overuse painkillers, stick to prescribed doses, and tell your doctor about everything you take.
  • Be wary of unreliable herbal supplements, as some may harm the liver despite being marketed as good for it.

When Should You See a Doctor?

If your tests show raised liver enzymes, the first step is to show the result to your doctor, who decides what it means in light of your health history and the medicines you take. They may ask you to repeat the test after a while, or to have further investigations to find the cause.

See a doctor without delay if jaundice appears, or severe abdominal pain, or severe unexplained tiredness, or any of the warning signs we mentioned. Do not try to diagnose or treat yourself based on the numbers alone.

Frequently asked questions

Does a rise in liver enzymes mean I have a serious disease?

Not necessarily. Many rises are mild and temporary, and are often caused by fatty liver. But the numbers alone are not enough, and the doctor is the one who decides whether it needs follow-up or further tests.

Can fatty liver improve and the enzymes return to normal?

Yes, in many cases fatty liver improves with losing excess weight, a balanced diet, physical activity, and reducing alcohol. Following up with your doctor helps show how much it has improved through repeat tests.

Can I have a liver problem without any symptoms?

Yes, many liver diseases are silent in their early stages and cause no clear pain. This is why regular checks are advised, especially for those with risk factors such as excess weight, diabetes, or viral hepatitis.

Are herbal supplements always safe for the liver?

No, some herbal supplements can harm the liver despite being marketed as good for it. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before taking any supplement or medicine, and tell them about everything you use.
