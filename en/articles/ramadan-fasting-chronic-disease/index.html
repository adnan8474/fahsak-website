Fasting Ramadan Safely with a Chronic Condition

By Adnan Alrefai · August 6, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • See your doctor well before Ramadan to adjust the timing and doses of your medications for fasting.
  • Make suhoor balanced and as late as possible, and drink enough water between iftar and suhoor.
  • Avoid overeating, too many sweets, and too much salt at iftar, and start light before your main meal.
  • Learn the signs of low blood sugar, and break your fast at once if you feel shaky, sweaty, dizzy, or confused.
  • Breaking the fast for health reasons is allowed and safe, and your health is a trust worth protecting.

Why you need to see your doctor before Ramadan

Fasting changes the timing of your meals, drinks, and sleep, and this directly affects how your medicines work in your body. A person with a chronic condition who takes medication for diabetes, blood pressure, or another illness often needs a change in dose or timing to keep fasting safe.

The best time for this review is a few weeks before Ramadan begins, not on its first day. Your doctor can then assess your condition, check how stable your illness is, decide whether fasting is suitable for you, and plan how to spread your medicines across suhoor and iftar.

Do not adjust your doses or medication times on your own, or based on what a relative or friend did, because every case is different. A personal decision based on your own examination is the safest and most correct one.

General tips for healthy fasting: suhoor and iftar

Make suhoor a balanced meal and as late as possible before dawn, because this helps steady your energy and blood sugar through the fasting hours. Choose foods that keep you full longer, such as whole grains, legumes, eggs, and yogurt, along with vegetables and a little healthy fat.

At iftar, start with a date and water or a light soup, then give your stomach a few minutes before the main meal. This protects you from overeating and from a sudden rise in blood sugar, and leaves you feeling more comfortable.

  • Split iftar into two stages: a light start, then a moderate meal a little later.
  • Eat plenty of vegetables and protein, and cut back on fried and greasy foods.
  • Go easy on Ramadan sweets and sugary drinks to avoid blood sugar spikes.
  • Chew slowly and stop when full, and do not make up for the fast with one huge meal.

Hydration: how to drink enough water

Dehydration is one of the most common problems during fasting, especially on hot or long days. The answer is not to drink a large amount all at once, but to spread water across regular intervals between iftar and suhoor.

Cut back on drinks that increase urination, such as coffee, strong tea, and fizzy drinks, because they can make you lose more fluid. Be careful with extra salt in your food too, as it increases thirst during the day.

Watch for signs of dehydration such as dizziness, dry mouth, passing little urine or dark urine, and unusual tiredness. If these signs clearly appear, you may need to break your fast and drink fluids.

  • Drink a cup or two at iftar, then spread the rest of your needs until suhoor.
  • Make water the base of your hydration, with soup and water-rich fruit as extras.
  • Avoid too much salt, pickles, and heavily spiced food at iftar and suhoor.

Diabetes: watch for low and high blood sugar

During fasting, a person with diabetes faces two dangers: low blood sugar (hypoglycemia), which is the more serious and can come on suddenly, and high blood sugar after iftar, especially with overeating and sweets. This is why your medicines, particularly insulin and some diabetes pills, must be reviewed with your doctor before Ramadan.

Signs of low blood sugar include shakiness, cold sweating, dizziness, a fast heartbeat, intense hunger, and confusion or difficulty concentrating. If you feel any of these, break your fast at once even before the call to prayer, take fast-acting sugar such as juice or a spoon of honey, then ask for help if needed.

Breaking the fast here is not weakness or failure, it is what your safety requires. Check your sugar during the day if you have a meter, and do not insist on completing the fast if your readings are very low, very high, or unstable.

  • Break your fast at once if your sugar drops sharply or signs of a low appear.
  • Always carry a source of fast-acting sugar for emergencies.
  • Break your fast too if your sugar rises very high or you feel severe thirst and dizziness.
  • Check your sugar when needed, as testing does not break the physical need and reassures you about your state.

High blood pressure: timing, salt, and fluids

Most people with high blood pressure can fast safely if their pressure is stable, but the key is adjusting medication timing. Some blood pressure medicines are taken once a day and can be moved to suhoor or iftar, while others are taken twice and need careful spacing, and your doctor decides this for you.

Excess salt raises pressure and increases thirst, so cut down on salty foods, pickles, and canned goods at suhoor and iftar. In return, make sure you hydrate well between the two meals, because low fluids can affect your pressure and cause dizziness when you stand.

Watch for symptoms such as a severe lasting headache, strong dizziness, blurred vision, or heart palpitations. If these symptoms clearly appear, it is better to break your fast and see your doctor rather than ignore them.

  • Do not skip or delay your pressure dose on your own, ask your doctor for a new schedule.
  • Reduce salt and pickles, and check your pressure regularly during the month if you can.
  • Drink enough water between iftar and suhoor to avoid low pressure and dizziness.

Conditions that need a special talk before deciding to fast

Not all chronic conditions are the same, and some need a special discussion with your doctor about whether fasting is suitable at all, and how. These include pregnancy and breastfeeding, kidney disease especially with dialysis, and diabetes that is poorly controlled or that recently had serious complications.

The list also includes those with frequent episodes of low blood sugar, people whose illnesses need medicines at precise times or steady water intake, and frail older adults. Only your doctor can balance your wish to fast against protecting your health.

This article is general health education and does not replace a consultation with your doctor, and it gives no religious ruling. The decision about whether to fast combines your doctor's guidance for your specific case with your own peace of mind, and your health is a trust worth protecting.

When to break your fast at once: warning signs not to ignore

Some symptoms mean your body is asking you to stop fasting in that moment, not to wait until sunset. Learn them in advance so you can act quickly and confidently when they happen, because delay can turn a small problem into a danger.

Breaking the fast in these cases is the right action to protect your life, and there is nothing to feel embarrassed about. Once you have settled, see your doctor so they can understand what happened and adjust your plan before you fast again.

  • Shakiness, cold sweating, dizziness, or confusion, which are signs of low blood sugar.
  • Severe thirst and clear dehydration with dizziness or near-fainting, which are signs of dehydration.
  • A severe lasting headache, blurred vision, or strong palpitations.
  • Chest pain, shortness of breath, or sudden weakness on one side of the body, so seek emergency help at once.

Frequently asked questions

Can a person with diabetes fast during Ramadan?

Many people with diabetes can fast safely if their sugar is stable and their medications have been reviewed with their doctor before Ramadan. But some cases, such as poorly controlled diabetes or someone with frequent low blood sugar episodes, may be advised not to fast. Your doctor decides this based on your condition.

Does checking my blood sugar or blood pressure break the fast?

This article gives health guidance and does not issue religious rulings, and from a health point of view, checking your readings is essential for your safety and should not be delayed out of worry about the fast. For any religious question you can ask a scholar, but medically, regular monitoring protects you and helps your doctor.

When must I break my fast at once for health reasons?

Break your fast at once if you feel shaky, break into a cold sweat, feel dizzy or confused (signs of low blood sugar), or have severe dehydration and near-fainting, a severe headache with strong palpitations, or chest pain and shortness of breath. Breaking the fast to protect your health is allowed and safe, then see your doctor to find the cause.

How do I spread my medications between suhoor and iftar?

Do not change your medication times or doses yourself. See your doctor before Ramadan to reschedule your medicines to fit the fasting hours, as some move to suhoor, some to iftar, and some need a dose change. The schedule your doctor sets specifically for you is the safest.

This content is for health education only and is not a substitute for medical advice. If you have symptoms that worry you, see your doctor.
