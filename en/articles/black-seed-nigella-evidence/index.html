Black seed (habbat al-barakah): the real evidence and the interactions that matter

By Adnan Alrefai · 14 July 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Nigella sativa contains thymoquinone, a compound that has shown anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in laboratory and animal studies.
  • Systematic reviews of clinical trials show a modest reduction in blood sugar and blood pressure in some participants, but the evidence base remains limited.
  • Because black seed can genuinely lower blood sugar and blood pressure, it can interact with diabetes and blood pressure medications and amplify their effect.
  • It should not be given to children under two, avoided in high doses during pregnancy, and your doctor must know if you use it alongside any prescribed medicine.
  • It has a real and growing research base, but it does not treat chronic diseases on its own and cannot replace prescribed treatment.

What is black seed and what does it contain?

Black seed, known in Arabic as habbat al-barakah or al-habbah al-sawda, is the seed of Nigella sativa, a plant referenced in prophetic medicine as a cure for every illness except death. It is cultivated across Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Syria and the Levant and has been used for thousands of years in cooking and traditional medicine. Its presence in Arab households is longstanding: mixed with butter and honey, added to bread and pastries, or infused in olive oil and used as a skin salve.

The most studied active compound in black seed is thymoquinone, which makes up roughly 30 to 48 percent of the essential oil extracted from the seed. Laboratory and animal studies have shown that this compound has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and antibacterial properties. The gap between what happens in a test tube and what happens in a living human body is significant, and this is the central challenge in evaluating any herbal remedy. Many substances produce striking results in laboratory conditions and then fail completely in human clinical trials.

The oil, powder and whole seed have different concentrations of active compounds, and dose and duration both affect outcomes. Most clinical studies used between 1.5 and 3 grams per day of powdered seed or extracted oil, which is considerably more than a random teaspoon. The quality of products used in studies also varied, which makes comparing their results a careful exercise.

It is worth understanding that black seed is not a single standardised preparation. An aqueous extract differs from the volatile essential oil, which differs in turn from cold-pressed fixed oil. Products sold commercially as black seed oil vary considerably in their thymoquinone content from one batch to the next, which means that comparing your experience with someone else's may be meaningless unless you are using the same product at the same dose.

What do human studies actually show?

A 2019 systematic review published in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, which examined the effect of Nigella sativa on type 2 diabetes, found a statistically significant reduction in fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels compared to placebo groups. The researchers noted, however, that individual study samples were small and follow-up periods were short. A complementary meta-analysis published in Complementary Therapies in Medicine in 2017 estimated an average reduction in HbA1c of about 0.45 percent among type 2 diabetic participants. That is a genuine and meaningful improvement, but considerably less than what established diabetes medications achieve.

On blood pressure, a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis published in the Journal of Hypertension in 2016, drawing on data from multiple randomised trials, found that black seed produced a reduction of roughly 3 to 5 mmHg systolic and 2 to 3 mmHg diastolic on average. That reduction is real and documented, but it is not of the magnitude achieved by a prescribed blood pressure drug in a patient with established hypertension. Its clinical importance lies in the fact that it adds to whatever medication the patient is already taking, which is precisely why the interaction matters.

A 2021 review in Current Cardiology Reviews mapped multiple mechanisms thought to underlie this blood pressure effect, including actions on smooth muscle in arterial walls, on renal function and on calcium channel activity. Understanding the mechanism helps explain why interactions with drugs working through overlapping pathways are plausible rather than merely theoretical.

It is worth noting that many available studies were conducted in countries where black seed is used traditionally, such as Malaysia, Iran, Pakistan and Arab countries, which makes their findings more directly relevant to this readership than a study conducted on populations with no history of using it. Long-term studies that follow participants for several years with large samples remain scarce, and this gap prevents definitive clinical recommendations from being issued at this stage.

Drug interactions: the warning most people miss

Because black seed genuinely lowers blood sugar, taking it alongside diabetes medications such as metformin, glibenclamide or insulin may cause a sharp drop in blood glucose, known as hypoglycaemia. Sweating, trembling, sudden dizziness, intense hunger and anxiety may be signs of this. The concern is that some people, especially those who already experience recurrent low blood sugar episodes, may feel no warning before a significant drop.

The same applies to blood pressure medication. A patient who is already taking medicine to control their pressure and adds large amounts of black seed may experience a sudden blood pressure drop, especially on standing quickly or on a hot summer day when sweating already lowers pressure. This translates to dizziness, a fall risk and possible fractures, not merely mild discomfort.

There is also experimental evidence that thymoquinone affects platelet function and blood clotting. Anyone taking anticoagulant drugs such as warfarin, or daily low-dose aspirin, needs particular caution and must tell their doctor before starting any black seed supplement. In clinics that monitor INR levels for warfarin patients, the INR may shift without any dose change, exposing the patient to bleeding risk.

The deeper problem is that many patients do not tell their doctors about the herbs and supplements they take, assuming that something natural cannot interact with a medicine. But herb-drug interactions are a documented medical reality. Black seed specifically has measurable biological activity, which is exactly what makes it interesting and exactly what makes honest disclosure to your doctor non-negotiable.

Tell your doctor before using black seed if you take

  • Diabetes medications (metformin, insulin, sulphonylureas)
  • Blood pressure medications of any type
  • Anticoagulants such as warfarin
  • Daily low-dose aspirin
  • Any heart medication

Who should avoid or be cautious with black seed?

Pregnancy and high-dose black seed do not mix safely. In traditional medicine, the seed has historically been used to stimulate the uterus, and animal studies confirmed this effect. Using it as a spice in cooking in small amounts carries no known risk, but capsules, extracts and oil at therapeutic doses should be avoided during pregnancy.

Children under two should not be given black seed extracts, because the liver enzyme system is not fully developed in this age group and safety studies in young children are absent. People who already have recurrent low blood sugar or low blood pressure are among those most affected by the interactions described above.

Patients with kidney or liver disease should consult their doctor before regular use, because extracts are processed through both organs and any impairment in their function may change how a dose behaves.

Which claims have gone beyond the evidence?

Social media carries claims that black seed cures cancer, eliminates viruses and reverses chronic diseases. Laboratory studies found effects of thymoquinone on cancer cells in a petri dish, but the distance from that to a real cancer treatment in a living person is enormous and has not been bridged. No published clinical trial has shown that black seed treats cancer in humans.

Claims also circulate that it treats immune failure, infertility and joint disease. Some of these have tentative early research behind them, but they do not meet the standard required for a clinical recommendation. The difference between a preliminary study and a proven treatment is fundamental and disappears in commercial promotion.

Being honest about what the evidence does and does not show makes the genuine findings more credible. Black seed has a real research base in modest blood sugar and blood pressure reduction, which is useful and important. But that same usefulness demands caution when it is used alongside drugs that do the same thing.

Claims not yet supported by clinical evidence

  • Treating cancer of any type
  • Eliminating viruses or hepatitis infections
  • Treating infertility with proven effect
  • Replacing prescribed diabetes or blood pressure medications

How to use black seed safely if you choose to

The safest use of black seed is as a cooking spice, which is how it has been used traditionally across Arab and South Asian kitchens for centuries. Adding a teaspoon of whole seeds to bread or vegetables gives you the flavour and nutritional content without reaching the therapeutic doses studied in clinical trials.

If you want to use supplements or oil regularly, speak to your doctor first, agree on a clear dose, and continue monitoring your blood sugar and blood pressure, especially in the first few weeks. Do not rely on how you feel alone, as low blood sugar and low blood pressure do not always produce immediate symptoms.

If you buy extracts or oil, look for a product with a quality certificate or laboratory analysis, because unstandard herbal products can vary enormously in their thymoquinone content from one batch to the next, meaning you cannot know precisely what you are taking.

Black seed in Islamic tradition: honest respect

The prophetic hadith concerning black seed, which describes it as a cure for every illness except death, is part of the Islamic medical heritage. Muslim scholars, both traditional and contemporary, interpret this text in various ways, and it is important that this heritage not be turned into an absolute medical claim exploited by dishonest sellers.

Genuine respect for the Islamic medical tradition means applying its commitment to careful honest inquiry to study it seriously. That is what a number of Muslim researchers have done in the published trials. What they found is significant and scientifically interesting, but it does not support claiming a cure for every modern disease.

The balanced position takes what rigorous research has shown, is cautious about what it has not shown, and refuses the commercial exploitation of a sacred religious text to sell overstated products. That is not a contradiction of faith; it is an honest expression of it.

When to see a doctor urgently if you use black seed

Some symptoms warrant urgent medical attention when they appear in the context of using black seed alongside other medications. Sudden severe dizziness, heavy sweating with trembling, or unexpected muscle weakness may be signs of low blood sugar or a drop in blood pressure.

If you notice yellowing of the whites of the eyes or the skin, or persistent nausea after starting extracts, these are signs that need liver function assessment. Sudden allergic reactions such as skin redness, itching and difficulty breathing require you to stop immediately and go to an emergency department.

Stop and seek medical help if you develop

  • Sudden severe dizziness, sweating or trembling
  • Yellowing of the eyes or skin after starting extracts
  • Persistent nausea and pain in the upper abdomen
  • Sudden skin rash or difficulty breathing

Frequently asked questions

Can I use black seed with metformin?

This is a question for your doctor before you start. Both lower blood sugar, and combining them may cause hypoglycaemia. If your doctor agrees, monitor your blood sugar regularly in the first few weeks and know the signs of a low.

Is black seed safe during pregnancy?

Small amounts used as a spice in food are generally considered safe. Concentrated extracts, oil and therapeutic-dose capsules are best avoided during pregnancy because of the possible effect on the uterus shown in traditional use and animal studies.

What is the correct dose of black seed?

Most clinical studies used between 1.5 and 3 grams of powdered seed or equivalent extracted oil per day. There is no officially agreed dose, and commercial products vary significantly in how much active compound they contain per serving.

Does black seed cure cancer?

No clinical trial in humans has shown this. Laboratory studies found interesting effects of thymoquinone on cancer cells in a dish, but that is not the same as a proven treatment. Do not rely on it instead of established cancer therapy.

Does black seed interact with warfarin?

There is evidence of a possible effect on blood clotting. Anyone on warfarin should tell the doctor managing their INR levels before using black seed regularly. The interaction could affect how stable your INR remains.

Are black seed, nigella and habbat al-barakah the same thing?

Yes. Black seed, habbat al-barakah, kalonji, black cumin and charnushka are all common names for the seeds of Nigella sativa, which is the scientific name used in medical research.

Can black seed be given to infants?

Extracts and concentrated preparations should not be given to children under two years of age. The seeds as a cooking ingredient in usual quantities do not pose a known risk, but therapeutic doses are not appropriate for this age group.

Does black seed lower cholesterol?

Some studies have shown a modest reduction in LDL cholesterol and improvement in the lipid profile, but results across trials are not consistent. It may be a useful addition to a healthy lifestyle but is not a replacement for a statin when one is indicated.
