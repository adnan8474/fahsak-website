Honey: where the evidence is solid, where it is not, and who must never have it

By Adnan Alrefai · 22 July 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Honey is genuinely effective as a topical treatment for superficial wounds and some burns, supported by Cochrane systematic reviews.
  • Honey reduces nighttime cough in children over one year of age and helps them sleep, but it does not shorten the duration of illness.
  • Honey must never be given to children under twelve months because of the risk of infant botulism, which can cause life-threatening respiratory paralysis.
  • Honey contains sugars and raises blood glucose, even if its glycaemic index is slightly lower than white sugar, so it is not a free food for people managing diabetes.
  • Many commercial claims about honey go well beyond what studies support, especially claims about treating chronic diseases.

Honey and wounds: the area with the strongest evidence

The most thoroughly documented medical use of honey is wound treatment. A 2015 Cochrane systematic review covering 26 clinical trials and more than 3,000 participants found that honey shortened the healing time for superficial burns compared with conventional gauze, and that its effect was comparable to silver sulfadiazine cream, the standard topical treatment for burns.

Several mechanisms explain why honey works on wounds. Its natural acidity inhibits bacterial growth. Its high sugar content draws moisture away from the wound environment, effectively desiccating microorganisms. It produces small quantities of hydrogen peroxide with antiseptic properties, and it contains other compounds including defensin proteins and polyphenols that inhibit a range of bacterial species including some resistant strains.

Medical honey used in hospitals is not the same as table honey from a jar. Certified medical honey, such as Manuka honey from New Zealand and Australia or commercial products like Medihoney, has been sterilised by irradiation and standardised for quality and concentration. Ordinary table honey can contain spores and varies in its composition, though it remains a reasonable temporary first aid option when nothing else is available.

A 2026 review in the International Wound Journal examined postoperative applications of Manuka honey and found evidence of reduced inflammation and enhanced new tissue growth. The medical interest in honey is not folklore; it is a serious scientific investigation of a natural material with measurable and reproducible effects.

Honey for coughs: when it helps and when it does not

Nighttime cough in children over one year of age is a problem that worries parents and disrupts sleep for the whole family. An updated 2018 Cochrane review comparing honey with placebo and common cough preparations found that honey reduced nighttime cough and improved sleep quality more than the control group. A 2023 European systematic review published in the European Journal of Pediatrics confirmed this finding across multiple trials.

Why might honey soothe a cough? Researchers think its viscous texture coats the walls of the throat and reduces irritation, and that its sweet taste stimulates saliva and mucus secretion, moistening the airway. There is no evidence that honey shortens the duration of a cold or kills the causative virus, but improving sleep quality is not a small achievement since good sleep actively supports the immune system.

In adults, the clinical evidence is not strong enough to say that honey clearly outperforms over-the-counter cough medications. However, a tablespoon dissolved in warm tea with lemon at the first sign of a cold remains a safe and soothing choice for many people, and may reduce unnecessary recourse to antibiotics which do nothing for viral illness.

Critical warning: honey is forbidden for children under one year

  • Honey of all types is absolutely prohibited for children under 12 months
  • The danger is infant botulism from spores of Clostridium botulinum
  • A baby's intestines cannot prevent these spores from multiplying and producing their neurotoxin
  • The botulinum toxin can cause respiratory paralysis and death
  • Sterilised, organic and local honey are all equally prohibited under twelve months

Honey and blood sugar: what diabetic patients need to know

A common claim is that honey is better than sugar for people with diabetes. The more accurate picture is that honey contains fructose, glucose, water and small amounts of enzymes and minerals, and its glycaemic index (GI) ranges between 50 and 65, compared with roughly 65 to 70 for white sugar. The difference exists but is modest.

One tablespoon of honey contains approximately 17 grams of sugar and 60 calories. If a person with diabetes adds two tablespoons of honey to their tea instead of white sugar, they are still raising their blood glucose substantially. The most useful way to think about honey is as a sugar that carries some additional nutritional value, not as a freely safe alternative.

Where honey genuinely differs from refined sugar is in its content of antioxidant polyphenols, including flavonoids and phenolic acids that have been linked in research to anti-inflammatory effects. A comprehensive 2023 review in the journal Nutrients examined this carefully and concluded that these compounds are present in meaningful quantities, but that the amount of honey a person would need to consume daily to reach therapeutic concentrations of antioxidants would raise blood sugar well beyond any offsetting benefit.

Infant botulism: what every parent and grandparent must know

In 1979, researcher Samuel Arnon and colleagues published the first study in the Journal of Pediatrics to document the link between honey and infant botulism. Since then, every paediatric health authority worldwide has aligned on the same guidance: no honey in any form for children under twelve months.

The reason adults and older children are unaffected is that their established intestinal flora prevent Clostridium botulinum spores from multiplying. An infant's gut lacks this protective microbial diversity, allowing the spores to germinate, multiply and produce the neurotoxin. The toxin blocks nerve signals to muscles, which can paralyse the muscles of breathing.

Symptoms typically begin between three and thirty days after honey consumption and include sudden severe constipation, weakened feeding, an unusually faint cry, and reduced muscle tone throughout the body. If any of these signs appear in an infant who has received honey, go to an emergency department immediately. The condition is urgent even when early symptoms seem mild.

Go to the emergency department immediately if your infant shows

  • Sudden severe constipation unlike previous patterns
  • Sudden weakness in feeding or drinking
  • Unusually faint or weak cry
  • Noticeable floppiness or reduced muscle tone
  • Do not wait for symptoms to worsen

Which claims about honey go beyond the evidence?

Social media carries claims that honey treats cancer, kills viruses and benefits every disease. Laboratory studies have found effects of honey compounds on cancer cells in a dish, but as with all such findings, the distance from that to a proven treatment in a living human has not been crossed. No clinical trial demonstrates that honey treats cancer.

The claim that honey generally boosts the immune system is too broad to evaluate and is not supported by specific clinical evidence. The immune system is a complex biological process that is not regulated by a daily spoonful of honey. A genuine immune problem needs medical evaluation and targeted treatment.

The idea that eating local honey treats seasonal allergies by providing exposure to local pollen has not been supported by rigorous clinical trials. Serious allergic symptoms deserve specialist medical assessment and evidence-based treatment rather than a honey-based plan.

Claims that lack adequate clinical support

  • Treating cancer or tumours of any type
  • Killing viruses such as herpes or influenza
  • Treating seasonal allergies through local honey
  • Broadly and permanently boosting the immune system

Honey in Arab and Islamic food tradition

Honey occupies a significant place in both Islamic medicine and Arab culinary tradition. It is mentioned in the Quran in Surah An-Nahl: 'There emerges from their bellies a drink, varying in colours, in which there is healing for people.' It appears in prophetic medicine alongside olive oil and black seed, and it features in Yemeni flatbreads, Levantine tahini mixes, Ramadan sweets and celebratory dishes across the region. This long culinary use has not produced notable historical harm and there is no reason to regard it with suspicion.

Problems begin when honey is treated as a medicine rather than a food, and when people believe that more is always better. Two tablespoons of honey provide around 100 calories almost entirely from sugar, which is a real number to account for. Large daily amounts raise total calorie intake, increase total sugar consumption and displace higher-nutrition foods from the plate. Honey is a genuine flavour addition and contributes antioxidants to a diet, but it is not the foundation of one.

Sidr honey, thyme honey, acacia honey and Yemeni mountain honey each carry reputations for particular health benefits across Arab communities. Their polyphenol content and glycaemic properties do genuinely vary by floral source, since the active compounds in honey depend directly on the flowers the bees feed from. Some high-quality artisanal honeys have been studied individually. However, comparative clinical trials between varieties remain limited, and marketing commonly goes considerably beyond what the evidence supports. A high price does not confirm superior medical benefit.

If you apply honey to a wound and notice increasing swelling, spreading redness, red streaks extending from the wound, or a fever, these are signs of bacterial infection requiring antibiotic treatment that no topical application can replace. Contaminated wounds, animal bites, and wounds in patients with diabetes that are slow to heal need medical evaluation in all circumstances, regardless of what has been applied to them. Early medical treatment is always safer than hoping the wound resolves on its own.

How to use honey sensibly and safely

For superficial wounds and minor burns when first aid supplies are not available: clean table honey is an acceptable temporary measure. Wash the wound thoroughly first, apply a thin layer of honey with a clean tool, and cover with sterile gauze. Deep wounds, contaminated wounds, animal bites and wounds that do not heal within days all need medical evaluation regardless of honey use.

For coughs in children over one year: one small to large teaspoon just before bedtime is reasonable based on moderate-quality evidence. Do not mix it in very hot water since high heat degrades some active compounds. Remember that it does not shorten the duration of the illness.

For people with diabetes or those managing their weight: honey is not a forbidden food but it is not a free food. Count it within your daily carbohydrate allowance and monitor your blood glucose response to it specifically, as individual responses to the same food vary.

When does honey use require a doctor?

If you have applied honey to a wound and notice increasing swelling, spreading redness, red streaks extending from the wound site, or a fever, these are signs of infection requiring antibiotic treatment that honey cannot provide. Contaminated wounds, animal bites, and diabetic patients whose wounds are slow to heal need medical evaluation in all circumstances.

If a child under twelve months has received any amount of honey in error and you notice any change in muscle tone, feeding behaviour or cry quality, do not wait for more symptoms to appear. Infant botulism requires assessment at an emergency department. Early treatment makes a decisive difference to the outcome.

Frequently asked questions

Is honey safe for babies if it is raw or organic?

No. Honey is prohibited for children under 12 months regardless of whether it is raw, organic, sterilised or locally sourced. The risk is from Clostridium botulinum spores which can exist in any honey and which an infant's immature gut cannot resist.

Can honey be used directly on an open wound?

For superficial wounds and minor burns it is an acceptable temporary first aid measure. Clean the wound thoroughly first and apply a thin layer. Deep wounds, wounds that show signs of infection and wounds that do not heal within a few days need medical review.

Does honey help adults with coughs?

Strong clinical evidence exists for children over one. For adults the evidence is less robust, but a tablespoon in warm tea is safe and unlikely to cause harm unless you have diabetes or restrictions on sugar intake.

Does honey raise blood sugar?

Yes. Honey is a natural sugar with a glycaemic index only slightly lower than white sugar. It raises blood glucose and should be counted within the daily carbohydrate allowance for people with diabetes.

What is the difference between Manuka honey and regular table honey?

Manuka honey is produced from the Leptospermum plant in New Zealand and Australia and contains high concentrations of methylglyoxal, which gives it a stronger and more consistent antibacterial activity. Certified medical honey has also been irradiation sterilised. Regular table honey does not match it in documented medical effectiveness.

Does honey treat seasonal allergies?

There is no reliable clinical evidence for this. The idea that local honey provides pollen desensitisation is plausible in theory but has not been confirmed in rigorous trials. Serious seasonal allergies need specialist evaluation and evidence-based treatment.

How much honey is safe to eat daily?

The WHO recommends keeping free sugars below 10 percent of daily calorie intake. Two tablespoons of honey contain around 100 calories from sugar, which is a significant fraction of most adults' allowance. That is the sensible reference point.

Is honey safe during pregnancy?

Yes, honey is safe for pregnant adults. The restriction applies only to children under one year because it is the infant gut that cannot resist botulinum spores. A pregnant woman with gestational diabetes should count honey within her sugar allowance.
