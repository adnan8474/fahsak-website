Vitamin B12 Deficiency: What It Is, and How to Spot and Treat It

By Adnan Alrefai · August 6, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Vitamin B12 is essential for healthy nerves, for making red blood cells, and for producing energy in the body.
  • Common symptoms include fatigue, tingling or numbness in the hands and feet, pale skin, memory or mood changes, and a sore tongue.
  • Those most at risk include vegetarians and vegans, older adults, people taking metformin or acid-reducing medicines, and people with absorption problems.
  • Diagnosis relies on a blood test, sometimes with supporting tests, and it is the doctor who interprets the result and decides on treatment.
  • If left untreated, deficiency can cause nerve damage that may become permanent, so early follow-up with a doctor matters.

What Vitamin B12 Is and What It Does

Vitamin B12 is an essential vitamin that the body needs in small but very important amounts. It takes part in several vital functions, and the body cannot make it on its own, so we get it from food or supplements.

This vitamin plays a key role in three areas: nerve health by helping maintain the protective sheath around nerves, the formation of red blood cells that carry oxygen, and support for energy production inside cells.

When B12 is low for a while, these functions can be affected gradually, so a person may feel tired or develop nerve symptoms or signs of anaemia. This article is for health education and does not replace a doctor's advice.

Symptoms of Vitamin B12 Deficiency

Symptoms of deficiency usually develop slowly, and a person may not notice them at first because they resemble many common, general complaints. That is why it is wise not to ignore persistent symptoms, especially when several appear together.

The severity of symptoms varies from one person to another, and some relate to the blood while others relate to the nerves. Having one symptom does not necessarily mean B12 deficiency, and only a doctor can connect the symptoms with the test results.

  • General tiredness, fatigue and low energy.
  • Tingling, numbness or pins and needles in the hands and feet.
  • Pale skin or a slight yellowish tinge.
  • Poor memory and concentration or mood changes.
  • A sore, painful or red tongue.
  • Problems with balance or feeling unsteady when walking.

Who Is Most at Risk

Some people are more prone to B12 deficiency than others, either because their diet lacks it or because their bodies do not absorb it well. Knowing the risk factors helps you stay alert early.

If you fall into one of these groups, it is a good idea to discuss it with your doctor, who may suggest a periodic check or preventive steps depending on your situation.

  • Vegetarians and vegans who eat few or no animal products.
  • Older adults, because absorption of the vitamin can decline with age.
  • People taking metformin for diabetes or acid-reducing medicines for long periods.
  • People with absorption problems such as pernicious anaemia, gut conditions, or after stomach or bowel surgery.

How Vitamin B12 Deficiency Is Diagnosed

Diagnosis usually begins with a blood test that measures the vitamin B12 level, and the doctor may also look at a full blood count, because deficiency can show up as anaemia with red cells that are larger than normal.

In some cases the doctor may request additional supporting tests to clarify the picture or to look for the cause. Each laboratory has its own reference range, and the result is interpreted within your own health context.

Importantly, reading the numbers is not for you to do alone. The doctor is the one who links the result to the symptoms and medical history and decides whether there is a deficiency that needs treatment.

Food Sources of Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12 is found naturally mainly in animal products, so for those who can, keeping a varied diet is helpful. Plant foods do not contain it naturally unless they have been fortified with it.

For this reason, vegetarians and vegans often need fortified foods or supplements, and it is best to do this on the advice of a doctor or dietitian.

  • Meat of various kinds and liver.
  • Fish and seafood.
  • Eggs.
  • Milk and dairy products.
  • Some fortified foods such as breakfast cereals and fortified plant-based milk alternatives.

Treatment and Why Untreated Deficiency Matters

Treatment depends on the cause and severity of the deficiency. In some cases, improving the diet or taking B12 by mouth is enough, while in others, especially when there is an absorption problem, the doctor may prescribe injections. The doctor decides the form, the dose and the duration.

It is not advisable to start high-dose supplements on your own without a diagnosis, because this may delay finding the real cause or mask another problem.

Ignoring the deficiency for a long time can lead to nerve damage, and some of this damage may become permanent if treatment is delayed. That is why early diagnosis and follow-up with a doctor are important to avoid complications.

Frequently asked questions

Is fatigue always a sign of vitamin B12 deficiency?

No. Fatigue has very many causes and is not proof of B12 deficiency. If the tiredness is persistent or comes with other symptoms such as tingling or paleness, see your doctor to assess the situation and request the appropriate tests.

Can vegetarians get enough B12 from plant foods?

Plant foods do not contain B12 naturally, so vegetarians often rely on fortified foods or supplements. It is best to discuss this with a doctor or dietitian to choose the right approach.

Are injections always better than tablets?

Not necessarily. Tablets can be enough in many cases, while injections are preferred when there is an absorption problem. The doctor decides the most suitable form based on the cause and severity of the deficiency.

Does tingling improve after treatment?

Many symptoms improve with early treatment, but if the deficiency lasts a long time some nerve damage may become permanent. This is why early diagnosis and treatment with a doctor's follow-up are important.
