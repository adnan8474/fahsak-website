Mental Health in Syria: A Silent Burden After the War and How to Care for Yourself

By Adnan Alrefai · August 6, 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • The war years left a heavy mental burden, and studies among displaced Syrians point to high rates of post-traumatic stress, depression, and anxiety.
  • These are normal human responses to abnormal circumstances, not weakness, a flaw, or a lack of faith.
  • Signs include lasting sadness, disturbed sleep, intrusive memories, constant worry, irritability, withdrawal, and unexplained physical aches.
  • Simple steps help a great deal: a daily routine, connecting with people, sleep and movement, and limiting exposure to distressing news.
  • Seek specialist help when needed, and if you have thoughts of harming yourself, seek urgent help immediately.

An unseen burden: what do the numbers say?

Alongside the visible wounds, the war years in Syria left invisible wounds in people's minds. Reviews of studies carried out among displaced Syrians indicate that rates of post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and anxiety are far higher than usual in the general population, with some studies estimating them at several times the global rate.

World Health Organization estimates also indicate that mental disorders are clearly higher among conflict-affected communities than among others. The great majority of respondents in some surveys linked their distress directly to the war.

Studies also show that women are more prone to these symptoms, and that loss, displacement, and the loss of safety are factors that add to the weight. These are estimates, but they are enough to say that caring for mental health is not a luxury, but a real need.

  • Reviews among displaced Syrians show high rates of PTSD, depression, and anxiety.
  • International estimates: mental disorders are higher among conflict-affected communities.
  • The majority in some surveys linked their distress to the war.
  • Women, the bereaved, and the displaced are more affected.

Normal responses to abnormal circumstances

It is important to understand that mental suffering after harsh events is not a sign of weakness or a flaw in one's character, but a normal human response to what is abnormal. The body and mind react to danger, loss, and fear in understandable ways.

In our communities, talking about mental health is sometimes seen as a stigma, so people keep their suffering to themselves. But acknowledging what we feel is the first step toward recovery, not the opposite. Asking for help is courage, not weakness.

Signs that may point to psychological stress

Mental symptoms are varied and can appear in different forms, some in the emotions and some in the body. Having one or two symptoms does not necessarily mean an illness, but their persistence or their effect on your daily life is a reason to seek support.

Pay attention to children too, as they may express their distress differently, such as bed-wetting, intense fear, changed behavior, or excessive clinginess. Listening to them patiently is very important.

  • Lasting sadness or emptiness, or losing pleasure in things you used to enjoy.
  • Disturbed sleep, whether insomnia or oversleeping, and recurring nightmares.
  • Intrusive memories or images of painful events, or avoiding reminders of them.
  • Constant worry, tension, quick irritability, or difficulty concentrating.
  • Recurring physical aches with no clear medical cause.
  • Withdrawing from people or losing hope.

Simple steps to care for yourself

Despite the difficult conditions, there are small things within your reach that help ease the mental weight. They will not solve everything, but they build a steadier foundation day by day.

Start with what you can, even one step. A simple routine, connecting with those you trust, movement, and regular sleep all restore some balance to body and mind. And remember that asking others for support is part of caring for yourself, not a diminishment of it.

  • Keep a simple daily routine for sleep and meal times.
  • Stay in touch with people you trust and do not isolate yourself.
  • Make time to move, even a walk, and sleep as regularly as you can.
  • Limit following the news and distressing scenes, especially before sleep.
  • Try slow breathing or relaxation exercises when tense.
  • Look for meaning and support in family, community, and worship if it comforts you.

How to support someone you love

Sometimes those we love do not ask for help, so we notice their change without knowing how to act. The simplest thing you can offer is to listen without judging or minimizing their feelings. Just being there and listening with care can ease a great deal for them.

Gently encourage them to seek specialist help when needed, without pressure or insistence. Avoid phrases that minimize their suffering, such as forget about it or others have it harder. Be present and patient, because recovery needs time.

When and where to turn, and a message of hope

If symptoms persist or affect your ability to work, your relationships, or daily life, it is important to seek specialist help from a doctor or mental health professional whenever available. Asking for support is a step of strength, and recovery is possible even after harsh experiences.

And if you have thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, do not stay alone with these thoughts. Talk at once with someone you trust, seek urgent medical help, or go to the nearest emergency service. Your life is precious, and your pain deserves to be heard and treated.

Remember that this article is for general awareness only and does not replace a professional, and it is not an emergency service. Despite all you have been through, there is always someone who can help you, and the first step toward recovery begins when you allow yourself to ask for help.

Frequently asked questions

Does struggling mentally after the war mean I am weak?

Not at all. Mental suffering after harsh events is a normal human response to what is abnormal, and it is not weakness or a flaw. Acknowledging what you feel and asking for support is a sign of strength and the first step toward recovery.

When should I seek specialist help?

Seek help if symptoms persist or affect your work, relationships, or daily life, or if you feel you cannot cope with them alone. And if you have thoughts of harming yourself, seek urgent help immediately.

How do I help a child affected by the events?

Listen to them patiently and without judgment, reassure and hold them, and keep a simple routine that makes them feel safe. Children may express distress through behavior rather than words, and if the signs persist, consult a specialist in child mental health.

Can the app treat my mental health condition?

No. The Sihtak app is a tool for general awareness and tracking, not a substitute for a therapist or an emergency service. It can offer general information and encourage you to seek support, but real treatment is with a specialist, and urgent situations need immediate intervention.
