Kidney Function Tests: A Simple Guide to Creatinine, Urea, and eGFR

By Adnan Alrefai · August 6, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Your kidneys filter waste from the blood and balance water and salts, and kidney function tests show how well they are doing this job.
  • Creatinine and urea are waste products cleared by the kidneys, and a rise in the blood can point to reduced filtering, but a doctor interprets the result.
  • The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is a calculated number that reflects how well the kidneys work and helps group kidney health into general stages.
  • Chronic kidney disease is often silent in its early stages, and its most common causes are diabetes and high blood pressure.
  • Protecting your kidneys starts with controlling sugar and blood pressure, drinking water in moderation, being cautious with painkillers, and seeing a doctor when concerned.

What Do Your Kidneys Actually Do?

Your kidneys are two small organs, each about the size of a fist, sitting in your lower back on either side of the spine. Despite their small size, they do a huge job every day, filtering waste and extra fluid from your blood and removing it as urine.

The kidneys do more than filter. They also balance water and salts in the body, help control blood pressure, and keep minerals like potassium and sodium in balance. They also contribute to substances that support red blood cell production and bone health.

When the kidneys start to work less well, waste builds up in the blood over time, and fluid and salt balance can be disturbed. This is why kidney function tests are ordered, as they give your doctor a snapshot of how well this vital system is working.

Creatinine and Urea: What They Are and Why They Rise

Creatinine is a substance produced by normal muscle activity. It passes into the blood and is then cleared by the kidneys into the urine. Because a person produces it at a fairly steady rate, a higher blood level can suggest the kidneys are not filtering it as they should.

Urea is another waste product, formed when the body breaks down proteins, and it is also cleared by the kidneys. Its level can rise in situations such as reduced kidney function, but it is also affected by other factors like dehydration, the amount of protein in food, and some medicines, so it is read alongside other tests, not alone.

It is important to know that creatinine levels are affected by muscle mass, age, and sex, so what counts as normal for one person may differ for another. This is why reference ranges vary between laboratories, and your doctor is the one who links the numbers to your full picture.

  • A rise in creatinine and urea may point to reduced kidney filtering.
  • Dehydration and low fluid intake can raise values temporarily.
  • Excess protein or certain medicines can affect urea levels.
  • Large muscle mass can raise creatinine without any disease.

What Does eGFR Mean?

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is a number the laboratory usually calculates from the creatinine level together with factors such as age and sex. It estimates how much blood the kidneys clean per minute, giving a clearer picture of how well they work than creatinine alone.

In general, the higher the eGFR number, the better the kidneys are performing, and the lower it is, the more it may point to reduced function. Doctors use this number to group kidney health into general stages, which helps them follow the condition and decide on suitable next steps.

However, eGFR remains an estimate rather than an exact measurement, and it can be affected by temporary conditions or by factors specific to the body. So a single number is not interpreted on its own but is read with your other results and health history, and only your doctor can decide what it means for you.

Why Is Chronic Kidney Disease Often Silent?

One of the most challenging features of chronic kidney disease is that it often develops quietly, without clear symptoms in its early stages. The kidneys have a strong ability to compensate and may keep doing their job even if their efficiency drops partly.

As a result, a person can lose part of their kidney function before noticing any change, and complaints may appear only at more advanced stages. This is exactly why regular testing matters, especially for people with risk factors.

For this reason, people at higher risk, such as those with diabetes or high blood pressure, are advised to have regular kidney function tests as directed by their doctor, because early detection allows better steps to preserve the kidney function that remains.

Main Causes and Symptoms as It Advances

Diabetes and high blood pressure are among the most common causes of chronic kidney disease worldwide. Long periods of high blood sugar can damage the tiny blood vessels inside the kidneys, and uncontrolled high pressure strains these vessels and weakens their ability to filter.

There are other possible causes, such as some kidney infections, repeated or blocking urinary stones, certain inherited conditions, and long-term overuse of particular medicines. Identifying the cause is up to the doctor after suitable examination and tests.

In advanced stages, some symptoms may appear that deserve attention, but they are not specific to the kidneys alone, so they are not suitable for self-diagnosis. Possible symptoms include:

  • Persistent tiredness, general weakness, and poor concentration.
  • Swelling in the feet, ankles, or around the eyes.
  • Changes in the amount or color of urine, or waking to urinate at night.
  • Nausea, loss of appetite, or itchy skin.

How to Protect Your Kidneys in Daily Life

The most important step to protect your kidneys is to control the main risk factors. If you have diabetes or high blood pressure, sticking to your treatment plan and monitoring your readings regularly reduces the long-term strain on your kidneys.

Lifestyle also plays a big role. A balanced diet, less salt, regular physical activity, quitting smoking, and drinking water in moderation are all habits that support kidney and overall health. The right amount of water differs from person to person, so it is best to ask your doctor if you have a specific health condition.

Pay attention to medicines too. Some painkillers, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), can strain the kidneys when overused or when you are dehydrated. Do not take any medicine for long periods or in large doses without consulting your doctor or pharmacist.

  • Control your blood sugar and blood pressure and follow your treatment plan.
  • Reduce salt, choose a balanced diet, and keep a healthy weight.
  • Drink water in moderation according to your needs and your doctor's advice.
  • Avoid overusing painkillers and do not take them long term without advice.

When Should You See a Doctor?

See your doctor if you are in a higher-risk group, such as people with diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of kidney disease, to decide whether you need regular kidney function tests and when to have them.

Seek medical care if worrying symptoms appear, such as unexplained swelling, a clear change in the amount of urine, severe tiredness, or ongoing nausea. Do not rely on interpreting your test results yourself, as the numbers need to be read within your full health context.

Frequently asked questions

Does a high creatinine always mean kidney disease?

Not always. Creatinine can rise temporarily due to dehydration, large muscle mass, some medicines, or intense physical effort. That is why it is read alongside other tests and your health history, and only a doctor can decide whether there is a problem that needs follow-up.

What is a normal eGFR number?

In general, a higher eGFR points to better kidney performance, but what counts as normal varies with age and other factors. There is no single number that fits everyone, and reference ranges differ between laboratories, so interpreting the number remains your doctor's task within your full picture.

Does drinking a lot of water improve kidney function?

Drinking water in moderation is good for overall health, but more is not necessarily better and may not suit every health condition. The right amount differs from person to person, so it is best to ask your doctor about the amount that suits you, especially if you have a chronic condition.

Do painkillers harm the kidneys?

Some painkillers, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), can strain the kidneys when overused, when you are dehydrated, or with certain conditions. Occasional use at suitable doses is usually lower risk, but it is best to consult your doctor or pharmacist before long-term use.
