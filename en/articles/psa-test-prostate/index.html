PSA test: what a high result really means for your prostate, and what happens next

By Adnan Alrefai · 8 October 2026 · 9 min read

In short

  • PSA is a protein made by the prostate, and a raised level means the prostate needs a closer look, not that you have cancer.
  • Enlarged prostate, infection, recent ejaculation, cycling and a urinary catheter can all push PSA up.
  • PSA rises naturally with age, so a result that is high for a 45-year-old may be usual for a 70-year-old.
  • A raised result is normally repeated first, and in many centres an MRI scan now comes before any biopsy.
  • Screening men without symptoms is a personal decision best made with a doctor from about age 50, or earlier if your father or brother had prostate cancer.

What does a high PSA result mean?

A high PSA means your prostate is releasing more of this protein into your blood than expected. It is a signal to look more closely, not a diagnosis. Prostate cancer is one possible cause, but the most common reasons for a raised PSA are an enlarged prostate, which affects most men as they age, and inflammation or infection of the prostate. Something as ordinary as ejaculating or a long bike ride in the day or two before the test can also nudge the number up.

Most labs flag a result above 4.0 ng/mL as high. That threshold is a convention rather than a hard line. Some men with a PSA below 4 have cancer, and many men above 4 do not. Results between about 4 and 10 are often called a grey zone, where benign enlargement and cancer overlap a great deal. Above 10, the chance of a significant problem is higher, and specialist review should not be delayed.

So if your report shows a raised number, the right next step is rarely panic and rarely nothing. It is usually a repeat test under the right conditions, a urine test to rule out infection, and a conversation with a doctor about your age, your symptoms and your family history.

What is PSA and why does the prostate make it?

The prostate is a gland about the size of a walnut that sits below the bladder and wraps around the tube that carries urine out of the body. It makes part of the fluid in semen. PSA, short for prostate specific antigen, is a protein it produces to keep semen liquid. Almost all of it stays in the semen, but a small amount leaks into the bloodstream, and that is what the blood test measures in nanograms per millilitre (ng/mL).

PSA is specific to the prostate, not to cancer. Anything that makes the gland bigger, inflamed or disturbed lets more PSA leak out. A prostate cancer can do this, but so can a benign enlargement or a simple infection. That is the central weakness of the test, and the reason every raised result has to be interpreted rather than read off the page.

Your PSA also changes over time. A study of 471 healthy men in Minnesota, published in JAMA in 1993, found that PSA tracked both age and prostate size, rising by roughly 3 percent a year in a healthy 60-year-old. That gradual climb is why doctors look at the trend across several tests rather than reacting to one number in isolation.

What can raise PSA apart from cancer?

Benign prostatic enlargement, also called BPH, is the commonest cause of a raised PSA. A bigger gland simply makes more PSA. It is extremely common after 50, and it is not cancer and does not turn into cancer, although the two can exist together. Prostatitis, an inflammation or infection of the prostate, and urinary tract infections can push PSA up sharply, sometimes to levels well above 10, before it falls again once the infection has settled.

Everyday activities matter too. A Turkish study of 98 men, published in 2016, found that total PSA rose in the first hours after ejaculation before returning to baseline. An Australian study of 129 men aged 50 to 71 found that a long recreational bike ride raised PSA by an average of 9.5 percent, and the number of men above the standard cut-off rose from two before the ride to six after it. The NHS therefore advises avoiding ejaculation, vigorous exercise and cycling for 48 hours before the test.

Procedures on the urinary tract can also raise PSA, including a urinary catheter, a cystoscopy camera test or a prostate biopsy. If you have had a urine infection, the NHS advises waiting 4 to 6 weeks after it clears before testing. In much of the region antibiotics are sold over the counter, and a few days of tablets taken without a diagnosis can leave a half-treated infection that keeps PSA raised, so it is better to have a urine test first.

Some medicines work the other way and lower PSA. Finasteride and dutasteride, prescribed for an enlarged prostate and, in the case of finasteride, for male pattern hair loss, can roughly halve PSA after several months. If you take either, tell the doctor reading your result, because a normal-looking number may actually be raised for you. Do not stop either medicine before a test unless your doctor tells you to.

What is a normal PSA for your age?

Because the prostate grows with age, a single cut-off of 4.0 ng/mL suits some men better than others. The 1993 Minnesota study proposed age-specific limits, based on the 95th percentile of healthy men: up to 2.5 ng/mL in your forties, 3.5 in your fifties, 4.5 in your sixties and 6.5 in your seventies. Many urologists still use these figures as a rough guide.

Arab men appear to run lower. Researchers at King Saud University in Riyadh measured PSA in 7,814 Saudi men aged 30 and over, in one of the largest such studies in the region. The average was 1.24 ng/mL, and 90.5 percent of men had a PSA between 0 and 2.5. The 95th percentile was 3.8 ng/mL in men aged 60 to 70 and jumped to 6.9 ng/mL in men over 71. The authors concluded that Saudi men have lower PSA than the populations on which the usual cut-offs were built.

In practice this means two things. A PSA of 3.5 in a 48-year-old Arab man may deserve more attention than the word "normal" on the lab report suggests. And a PSA of 5 in a man of 75 with an enlarged prostate may not need the same urgency as it would in a younger man. Your doctor should weigh your result against your age rather than the single printed range.

Free PSA versus total PSA: what the ratio tells you

PSA circulates in two forms. Most of it is bound to other proteins in the blood, and a smaller share floats free. The standard test, total PSA, measures both together. Free PSA measures only the unbound part, and labs usually report it as a percentage of the total, printed as free to total ratio or % free PSA.

The ratio is useful mainly in the grey zone, when total PSA is somewhere between about 4 and 10. Cancer tends to produce a higher share of bound PSA, so a low free percentage raises the chance that cancer is the cause, while a high free percentage points more towards benign enlargement. The exact cut-off differs between labs, methods and guidelines, so it is your urologist, not the printout, who should decide what your ratio means.

Free PSA is not a test to request on its own or to repeat endlessly. It is one piece of a wider assessment that also includes your age, the size of your prostate, your family history, a rectal examination and, increasingly, an MRI scan. Some specialists also calculate PSA density, which is the PSA divided by the prostate volume measured on a scan, because a moderately raised PSA in a very large gland is less worrying than the same number in a small one.

Should you have a PSA test if you have no symptoms?

This is an honest debate, and it is worth understanding both sides. The largest trial, the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer, followed 162,236 men aged 55 to 69 for a median of 23 years and reported its final results in 2025. Men invited for regular PSA testing were 13 percent less likely to die of prostate cancer. One death from prostate cancer was prevented for every 456 men invited to screening.

The cost of that benefit is overdiagnosis. Screened men were diagnosed with prostate cancer 30 percent more often, and many of those cancers would never have caused symptoms or shortened life. Diagnosis can lead to biopsies, anxiety and treatments with side effects such as erection problems and urine leakage. That is why most guidelines do not recommend testing every man automatically, and why the NHS does not run a routine screening programme.

The usual advice is a shared decision with your doctor from about age 50, having weighed the possible benefits and harms. Men at higher risk are often advised to start that conversation earlier, from around 45, including men whose father or brother had prostate cancer, men of Black African descent and men known to carry a BRCA2 gene change. In many Arab countries PSA is added to men's check-up packages at private labs without any discussion, so you may receive a result before you have thought about what you would do with it. It is worth having that conversation before the blood is taken.

What happens after a raised PSA result?

The first step is almost always a repeat test, taken after 48 hours without ejaculation, cycling or hard exercise, and ideally at the same lab so the results are comparable. A urine test checks for infection. Your doctor may also examine your prostate with a gloved finger through the back passage, which takes less than a minute and tells them about its size, texture and any hard areas.

If PSA stays raised, the modern next step in many centres is an MRI scan of the prostate before any biopsy. The PRECISION trial, published in 2018, randomised 500 men with suspected prostate cancer. In the MRI group, 28 percent had a scan that did not suggest cancer and avoided a biopsy altogether, and significant cancers were found more often, in 38 percent compared with 26 percent of men who had a standard biopsy. MRI also led to fewer diagnoses of harmless cancers.

If the MRI shows a suspicious area, a biopsy takes small samples of tissue with a fine needle, often aimed at that area. A pathologist examines them and grades any cancer found. Many low-grade cancers are now managed by active surveillance, meaning regular PSA tests and scans with treatment only if the cancer changes, rather than immediate surgery or radiotherapy.

Prostate MRI is not available in every city in the region, and where it exists it can be costly. If it is not available to you, ask the urologist what the alternatives are and whether a repeat PSA after a few months is a reasonable way to watch the trend before deciding on a biopsy.

Symptoms of an enlarged prostate, and when to get urgent help

Because the prostate surrounds the urine tube, a bigger gland can squeeze it. Typical symptoms include getting up at night to pass urine, needing to go often or urgently, a weak or stop-start stream, difficulty getting started, dribbling at the end and a feeling that the bladder has not emptied. A short questionnaire called the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) scores these symptoms, it has been translated into Arabic and tested in the region, and your doctor may ask you to fill one in.

These symptoms are very common and are usually caused by benign enlargement, not cancer. Early prostate cancer often causes no symptoms at all, which is part of why the screening debate exists. Effective treatments for enlargement are available, from medicines that relax the prostate to procedures that widen the channel, so urinary symptoms are worth raising with a doctor even if your PSA is normal.

Some symptoms cannot wait for a routine appointment. Being unable to pass urine at all, with a painful, swollen lower belly, is an emergency because the bladder needs draining. Fever and shivering with burning urine and pain between the legs can mean acute prostatitis, which needs same-day treatment. Visible blood in the urine always deserves prompt assessment.

Go to an emergency department or see a doctor today if

  • You cannot pass urine at all and your lower belly is painful or swollen
  • You have fever or shivering with burning urine or pain between the legs
  • You see blood or clots in your urine
  • You have new back or hip pain with weight loss you cannot explain
  • You have back pain with new weakness, numbness in the legs, or loss of bladder or bowel control

Frequently asked questions

Does a high PSA mean I have prostate cancer?

No. A raised PSA means the prostate needs a closer look, but the most common causes are benign enlargement and infection or inflammation. Most men with a raised PSA do not have cancer. A repeat test, a urine test and a doctor's assessment come first.

What is a normal PSA level for my age?

A widely used guide gives upper limits of about 2.5 ng/mL in your forties, 3.5 in your fifties, 4.5 in your sixties and 6.5 in your seventies. A large Riyadh study found that Saudi men tend to have lower PSA than these figures, with an average of 1.24 ng/mL. Your doctor should read your result against your age.

What should I avoid before a PSA test?

The NHS advises avoiding ejaculation, vigorous exercise and cycling for 48 hours before the test, as all can raise PSA slightly. You can eat and drink normally. If you have had a urine infection, wait 4 to 6 weeks after it clears.

What does a low free PSA percentage mean?

Free PSA is the part not bound to other proteins. When total PSA is in the grey zone of about 4 to 10, a lower free percentage makes cancer more likely and a higher one points towards benign enlargement. Cut-offs vary between labs, so a urologist should interpret it.

At what age should I start having PSA tests?

Most guidelines suggest discussing PSA testing with a doctor from about age 50. Men whose father or brother had prostate cancer, men of Black African descent and carriers of a BRCA2 gene change are often advised to start that discussion from about 45. It is a choice, not an automatic test.

Can finasteride for hair loss affect my PSA?

Yes. Finasteride and dutasteride can lower PSA by roughly half after several months of use. Tell the doctor reading your result that you take one of them, so the number can be interpreted correctly. Do not stop the medicine without your doctor's advice.

Will my PSA go down after a prostate infection?

Usually yes. Infection and inflammation can raise PSA sharply, and it generally falls back over several weeks once the infection is treated. That is why doctors repeat the test 4 to 6 weeks after an infection has cleared rather than acting on a reading taken during it.
