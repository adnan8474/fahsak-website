High Blood Pressure: Why It Is Called the Silent Killer and How to Take Control

By Adnan Alrefai · August 6, 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Blood pressure is measured with two numbers: systolic (the top) and diastolic (the bottom), written like 120/80.
  • High blood pressure often has no symptoms, which is why it is called the silent killer, and a doctor confirms the diagnosis over several readings.
  • Long-term high pressure strains the heart, kidneys and brain and raises the risk of stroke and heart attack.
  • Simple steps make a real difference: less salt, weight loss, activity, quitting smoking, good sleep and managing stress.
  • A reading of 180/120 or higher with symptoms like chest pain, shortness of breath or blurred vision needs emergency help right away.

What Do Blood Pressure Numbers Mean?

Blood pressure is the force your blood pushes against the walls of your arteries as it moves around the body. It is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and always written as two numbers, the top over the bottom, like 120/80.

The top number is the systolic pressure, the force of the blood when your heart contracts and pushes it out. The bottom number is the diastolic pressure, the pressure between beats when your heart relaxes and refills with blood.

Both numbers matter. One can rise while the other stays within range, and together they give your doctor a picture of the health of your heart and arteries. There is no single perfect number for everyone, but a value near 120/80 is a good reference for most adults.

The General Blood Pressure Categories

General categories are used to describe blood pressure readings, but they are only a guide. Your doctor confirms the diagnosis based on several readings taken at different times, not on a single measurement.

Do not panic over one high reading. Pressure can rise temporarily due to stress, coffee or exertion. What matters is the repeated pattern over time, and that is what your doctor evaluates.

  • Normal: below 120 systolic and below 80 diastolic.
  • Elevated: systolic between 120 and 129 with diastolic below 80.
  • Stage 1: systolic between 130 and 139, or diastolic between 80 and 89.
  • Stage 2: systolic 140 or higher, or diastolic 90 or higher.
  • A reading of 180/120 or higher is dangerous and needs urgent medical attention.

Why Is It Called the Silent Killer?

High blood pressure is called the silent killer because in most cases it causes no obvious symptoms. A person can live for years with high pressure and feel nothing while it quietly damages the blood vessels.

The belief that a headache or a flushed face is a sure sign of high pressure is mistaken. Many people with it feel nothing at all, which is why regular measurement is the only reliable way to catch it early.

Over time, untreated high pressure raises the risk of serious problems in the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes. The good news is that finding and controlling it early greatly reduces these risks.

What Harm Does It Cause Over the Long Term?

Sustained high pressure forces the heart to work harder to pump blood, which over time can lead to enlargement and weakening of the heart muscle and increase the risk of heart attack and heart failure.

It also damages the arteries, making them narrower and stiffer, which raises the risk of stroke. It affects the kidneys, weakening their ability to filter waste, and can harm the tiny vessels of the eye and affect vision.

  • Heart: heart attack, heart failure, enlarged heart muscle.
  • Brain: stroke and weakened memory and thinking.
  • Kidneys: reduced kidney function up to kidney failure.
  • Eyes: damage to retinal vessels and vision problems.

How to Measure Your Pressure Correctly at Home

Home measurement is a valuable tool, but its accuracy depends on your technique. Use a reliable device with a cuff that wraps around the upper arm (not usually the wrist), and make sure the cuff size fits your arm.

Rest for five minutes before measuring, sit with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor without crossing your legs, and rest your arm on a table so the cuff is at heart level. Avoid caffeine, smoking and exercise for at least half an hour beforehand, and do not talk during the measurement.

  • Measure at the same time each day, morning and evening if you can.
  • Take two readings one to two minutes apart and record the average.
  • Do not rely on a single reading, but on a pattern over several days.
  • Write down the numbers with the date and time to show your doctor.

Lifestyle and Medication: Your Tools for Control

Many people can lower their pressure through lifestyle changes, and sometimes with medication alongside them. Cutting salt is one of the most powerful levers, and hidden salt in bread, processed foods, canned goods and ready meals adds up to far more than what we sprinkle on our plates.

Losing excess weight, walking or moving on most days of the week, quitting smoking, limiting alcohol, sleeping enough and managing stress all improve your chances of control. Eating plenty of vegetables, fruit and whole grains helps too.

If your doctor prescribes a medication, taking it consistently matters even when you feel fine, because the pressure will rise again if you stop. Do not stop the medication or change the dose on your own, and if it causes bothersome side effects, tell your doctor so the treatment can be adjusted rather than stopped.

When Should You Seek Emergency Help?

Most high blood pressure is managed calmly with a doctor, but some situations are emergencies. If you measure your pressure and find it at 180/120 or higher, measure again after a few minutes. If it stays this high and is accompanied by symptoms, this is an emergency and you should call for an ambulance right away.

Serious warning signs include chest pain or pressure, severe shortness of breath, weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, blurred or lost vision, a sudden severe headache, or confusion. Do not wait in these situations and seek urgent medical help.

  • A reading of 180/120 or higher with any warning sign.
  • Chest pain or severe shortness of breath.
  • Sudden weakness or numbness, or trouble speaking or seeing.
  • A sudden severe headache, confusion or seizures.

Frequently asked questions

Can I tell that my blood pressure is high from symptoms?

Usually not. Most people feel no symptoms at all, which is why it is called the silent killer. The only reliable way is regular measurement, and a doctor confirms the diagnosis over several readings.

Does one high reading mean I have high blood pressure?

Not necessarily. Pressure can rise temporarily due to stress, caffeine or exertion. Diagnosis is based on a repeated pattern of high readings at different times, confirmed by a doctor, not on a single measurement.

Can I stop my blood pressure medication once my readings improve?

Do not stop it on your own. Often the readings are good precisely because the medication is working, and stopping it can let the pressure rise again. Always talk to your doctor before any change in dose or treatment.

How often should I measure my blood pressure at home?

It depends on your situation and your doctor's advice. Many people measure morning and evening at the same time, taking two readings and recording the average. What matters most is regular monitoring and writing down the numbers to show your doctor.
