Blood Thinners: Warfarin, the Newer Drugs and How to Stay Safe

By Adnan Alrefai · 3 August 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Warfarin requires regular INR monitoring and is sensitive to food and drug interactions. The target INR range depends on why you are taking it.
  • Newer anticoagulants (DOACs) such as apixaban and rivaroxaban do not need routine INR monitoring but cost more, are less available in the region, and are not suitable for all conditions.
  • The target INR is 2 to 3 for atrial fibrillation and venous clots, and 2.5 to 3.5 for mechanical heart valves.
  • Foods rich in vitamin K such as spinach, parsley and dark leafy greens affect warfarin. The answer is to eat them consistently, not to avoid them.
  • Tell every doctor and dentist that you take a blood thinner before any procedure. Stopping or continuing requires a medical decision.

Why are blood thinners prescribed?

Blood thinners, correctly called anticoagulants, slow the blood's clotting process. They do not dissolve existing clots but prevent new ones from forming and stop existing ones from growing. They are prescribed for several conditions that share the risk of clot formation in situations where it causes harm.

The most common reason in the region is atrial fibrillation, a heart rhythm disorder in which the upper chambers of the heart beat chaotically. This causes blood to pool and form clots that can travel to the brain and cause a stroke. Anticoagulants substantially reduce this stroke risk. They are also prescribed for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, for people with mechanical heart valves, and occasionally after certain types of heart attack.

Warfarin has been the standard anticoagulant in the region for decades because it is inexpensive and widely available. Over the past decade, a class of newer drugs called direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) including apixaban, rivaroxaban and dabigatran has become available. They work differently from warfarin and are easier to manage, but they are substantially more expensive and less available in parts of the Middle East and North Africa.

What is INR and how do you read your result?

INR stands for International Normalized Ratio. It is a standardised measure of how long it takes your blood to clot compared to a person not on any anticoagulant medication. A person not taking warfarin has an INR of approximately 1. The higher the INR, the less readily your blood clots, and the greater the risk of bleeding.

The target INR depends on why you are taking warfarin. For most people with atrial fibrillation or venous thromboembolism, the target range is 2.0 to 3.0. For people with mechanical heart valves the range is typically 2.5 to 3.5. Your doctor specifies your personal target based on your specific diagnosis. An INR above 4.0 indicates a meaningfully increased bleeding risk and needs a dose review. An INR below 2.0 in someone being treated for atrial fibrillation means they are not getting adequate protection against stroke.

A nationwide cohort study published in Annals of Medicine in 2024 examined large numbers of patients on warfarin and confirmed that the proportion of time spent within the therapeutic range is directly associated with outcomes. Patients who spend most of their time in range have substantially fewer strokes and major bleeds than those whose INR fluctuates above and below the target. This is the main argument for regular monitoring and for understanding what makes INR change.

Foods that change how warfarin works

Warfarin works by blocking the action of vitamin K in producing clotting factors. When you eat large amounts of vitamin K-rich foods, more vitamin K reaches the liver and partly counteracts warfarin, causing INR to fall. When you suddenly reduce your intake of these foods, vitamin K falls and warfarin becomes more powerful, causing INR to rise. Both shifts increase your risk.

A review published in Current Opinion in Clinical Nutrition and Metabolic Care in 2007 examined this interaction carefully and reached a conclusion that practitioners still rely on: consistency in vitamin K intake matters far more than avoidance. The goal is not to stop eating spinach, parsley, coriander, dark lettuce or kale, but to eat them in roughly the same amounts from week to week. A sudden decision to eat a large amount of spinach after avoiding it for weeks is what causes a clinically significant drop in INR.

In the Arab kitchen, vitamin K is concentrated in fresh parsley and coriander used in large quantities, as in tabbouleh or certain stews, dark leafy vegetables, and some herbal preparations. Green tea also contains vitamin K. The practical approach is to eat what you normally eat and avoid large fluctuations. If you make a significant dietary change, tell your doctor and arrange an INR check within a week.

Foods that affect warfarin: eat consistently, not less

  • Spinach, parsley, coriander, dark lettuce: keep amounts stable week to week
  • Cabbage and broccoli: fine to eat but sudden large increases affect INR
  • Green tea: keep daily amounts consistent
  • Grapefruit juice: can affect some DOACs, ask your doctor

Medicines that interact with blood thinners

Dozens of commonly prescribed medicines raise or lower the effect of warfarin. Some increase its activity and raise bleeding risk; others reduce it and leave you less protected. The most clinically important interactions include aspirin and all non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen and diclofenac, which add an independent bleeding risk through a different mechanism. Antibiotics of many classes affect INR, particularly those that reduce gut bacteria which produce some vitamin K.

A 2022 systematic review in Pharmacotherapy examined drug interactions with anticoagulants, specifically rifamycin antibiotics, and found that rifampicin substantially reduces the effect of warfarin and some DOACs. Rifampicin is widely used to treat tuberculosis, which remains prevalent across the region, making this a practically important interaction in the Middle Eastern context. Patients starting TB treatment while on anticoagulants need more frequent INR checks and often a significantly higher warfarin dose.

The practical rule: tell every prescriber and every pharmacist that you take an anticoagulant before any new medicine is added to your regimen, including over-the-counter medicines, supplements, and herbal preparations. Garlic, ginger, fish oil and ginkgo biloba all have antiplatelet effects that add to anticoagulant risk. Even a dose of aspirin bought without a prescription can meaningfully increase your bleeding risk if you are already on warfarin or a DOAC.

Warfarin versus DOACs: which is better for you?

The direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) target specific clotting factors rather than the vitamin K pathway. Apixaban and rivaroxaban inhibit factor Xa; dabigatran directly inhibits thrombin. They are taken in fixed doses with no routine blood monitoring needed, which makes adherence simpler for many patients.

A 2022 patient-level meta-analysis published in Circulation that pooled data from major clinical trials found that DOACs provide comparable stroke prevention to warfarin in atrial fibrillation, with a lower rate of intracranial bleeding, which is the most dangerous form of anticoagulation-related haemorrhage. A large multinational cohort study in Annals of Internal Medicine in 2022 comparing the four main DOACs found modest differences between them in specific outcomes, underscoring that choice should be individualised.

DOACs are not universally superior. They are contraindicated in people with mechanical heart valves, where warfarin remains the only evidence-based oral anticoagulant. Patients with severe kidney disease may not be able to take some DOACs safely. The absence of routine monitoring, while convenient, also means a missed dose or non-adherence goes undetected. And in settings where a reversal agent for a bleeding emergency may not be available, the lack of a universal antidote for some DOACs is a real consideration.

Bleeding warning signs

Bleeding is the main side effect of all anticoagulants. Most bleeding that occurs on these medicines is manageable, but some situations are emergencies. Knowing which is which helps you act appropriately rather than either panicking over a bruise or waiting too long with something serious.

Signs that need a medical review but are not immediately life-threatening include: new large bruises appearing without a clear cause; bleeding from the gums that lasts longer than usual after brushing; a small cut that takes a long time to stop. These suggest your INR may be running too high and warrant a test and possible dose adjustment.

Signs that require going to the emergency department the same day without delay include: blood in the urine turning it red or dark brown; blood in the stool or black, tarry stools; vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds; a sudden severe headache unlike any you have had before; weakness or numbness on one side of the body; difficulty speaking; sudden change in vision. Any of these requires emergency assessment immediately.

Go to the emergency department immediately if you notice

  • Blood in the urine, stool or vomit
  • Black or tarry stools
  • A sudden severe and unusual headache
  • Weakness or numbness down one side of the body
  • Sudden difficulty speaking or blurred vision

Before surgery, dental work or procedures

Blood thinners complicate procedures because they slow clotting at the site where the surgeon or dentist needs haemostasis to occur. The key is always to inform the medical team before any intervention, whether major surgery or a simple tooth extraction, so they can plan appropriately.

For major surgery, the surgeon and anaesthetist decide together when to pause the anticoagulant before the operation and whether bridging anticoagulation with injectable heparin is needed during the pause. This decision depends on the bleeding risk of the procedure and the clot risk of stopping anticoagulation. Never stop your anticoagulant before surgery on your own initiative without a direct instruction from your doctor.

For routine dental cleaning and simple dental extractions, current guidance indicates that most patients on warfarin with an INR in the therapeutic range can continue their warfarin without interruption. Local haemostatic measures such as pressure, sutures and haemostatic dressings are used by the dentist. Your dentist needs to know your current INR value before proceeding. DOACs are handled similarly: some guidelines suggest a brief pause before more invasive dental procedures, guided by the specific drug and the procedure's bleeding risk.

Living well on anticoagulants: travel, activity and daily life

Most people on long-term anticoagulation live a full and active life. The adjustments required are real but not limiting. Understanding them makes adherence easier and reduces anxiety about the medication.

When travelling, carry your medicine in hand luggage rather than checked bags. Carry a brief medical card in your wallet or phone stating the drug name, dose, and indication. If you travel to a country where your specific DOAC brand may not be available, take a sufficient supply for the entire trip plus a reserve. If your INR is due to be checked during a trip abroad and INR testing is not easily accessible, discuss this with your doctor before travel and agree on a plan.

Physical activity and exercise are encouraged, not contraindicated. Regular walking, swimming and moderate exercise pose no additional risk at therapeutic INR levels and have cardiovascular benefits. High-contact sports or activities with a significant fall risk need discussion with your doctor, particularly if INR is running at the higher end of your range. The answer is rarely to stop all activity: it is to choose activities that fit your current level of risk.

Frequently asked questions

What INR should I aim for on warfarin?

It depends on why you are taking warfarin. For atrial fibrillation and venous clots the standard target is 2.0 to 3.0. For mechanical heart valves it is usually 2.5 to 3.5. Your doctor sets your personal target. An INR above 4.0 increases bleeding risk and needs a dose review; below 2.0 means the protection is insufficient.

Do I need to avoid spinach and leafy vegetables on warfarin?

No, you do not need to avoid them. The key is consistency: eat roughly the same amounts of vitamin K-rich foods from week to week. Sudden large increases or drops in intake are what cause INR to shift. If you make a big dietary change, arrange an INR check within a week and let your doctor know.

Are DOACs safer than warfarin?

In atrial fibrillation, DOACs have shown similar stroke prevention to warfarin with a lower rate of intracranial bleeding in clinical trials. But they are not suitable for mechanical heart valves, can be problematic in severe kidney disease, are much more expensive, and require good adherence since there is no monitoring to catch missed doses. The better choice depends on your specific situation.

What should I do if I miss a dose?

For warfarin, if you remember the same day take it as soon as possible. If it is nearly time for the next dose, skip the missed one and continue normally. Never double up. For DOACs the advice varies by drug: apixaban missed doses have a slightly different rule from rivaroxaban, so ask your pharmacist or doctor for the specific guidance for your medication.

Can I take aspirin or ibuprofen with my blood thinner?

Both aspirin and ibuprofen add an independent bleeding risk on top of anticoagulants. Some patients need aspirin alongside an anticoagulant for specific cardiac reasons under medical supervision. Without a clear medical reason, avoid both. Paracetamol is generally the safer pain reliever for people on anticoagulants but note that large regular doses of paracetamol can also raise INR in warfarin users.

How often do I need my INR checked?

When warfarin is first started or when the dose is changed, INR needs checking every few days to a week until it stabilises. Once your INR is consistently in the target range, monthly or six-weekly checks are typical. Any illness, new medication, or significant dietary change should prompt an early check outside the regular schedule.

What should I tell the dentist before a tooth extraction?

Tell them you take an anticoagulant and bring your most recent INR reading if you are on warfarin. For most simple extractions in patients with therapeutic INR, current guidelines allow continuing warfarin with local haemostatic measures rather than stopping the drug. The dentist and your doctor together make that call, not you alone.

Can I travel abroad while on a blood thinner?

Yes, most people on anticoagulants travel without problems. Carry your medicine in hand luggage, bring a medical card stating the drug and dose, and take a generous supply that covers your whole trip. If INR monitoring will be difficult during a long trip, discuss a plan with your doctor before you go. Blood clots from long-haul flights are a real risk, so staying hydrated and moving regularly on the plane is especially important for you.
