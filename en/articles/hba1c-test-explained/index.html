The HbA1c Test: What Your Number Really Means

By Adnan Alrefai · August 6, 2026 · 6 min read

In short

  • The HbA1c test measures your average blood sugar over roughly three months, not a single moment.
  • It needs no fasting, and the blood sample can be taken at any time of day.
  • Guidelines worldwide use 5.7% and 6.5% as widely quoted cutoffs, but interpreting your number belongs to your doctor.
  • Some conditions such as anaemia, pregnancy, and certain blood disorders can skew the result.
  • Simple habits like a balanced diet, regular movement, and enough sleep can improve your number over time.

What does the HbA1c test measure?

The HbA1c test does not tell you your blood sugar at this very moment. Instead, it gives a picture of your average blood sugar over roughly the past three months. That is why it is sometimes called a mirror of your long-term sugar control.

The idea behind it is simple. Your blood carries a protein called haemoglobin inside the red blood cells, and some of the sugar in your blood sticks to this protein. The higher your sugar has been over recent weeks, the more of your haemoglobin ends up coated with sugar. Because a red blood cell lives for about three months, this percentage reflects your average sugar over that period, and the result is reported as a percentage.

That is what makes it so useful. A single reading sums up for your doctor how your sugar has been overall, rather than relying on one snapshot that can rise or fall with your last meal, your stress, or your sleep.

How is it different from a fasting glucose test?

Many people confuse this test with a fasting glucose test, even though each one measures something different. A fasting glucose test is like a photograph: it shows your sugar level at the moment of the draw, and usually needs several hours of fasting beforehand. HbA1c is more like a short film that sums up the average of recent months.

One of the biggest advantages of HbA1c is that it needs no fasting at all. You can eat normally, and the blood can be drawn at any time of day, because your last meal does not change the average it measures. That is a real convenience for anyone who struggles with long fasting.

Even so, one does not replace the other. Sometimes your doctor orders both so each completes the picture, with the fasting test showing the here and now and HbA1c showing the overall trend.

Why does your doctor order this test?

Doctors use the HbA1c test for two main reasons. The first is to help detect diabetes or prediabetes in people who have risk factors, such as being overweight, a family history of the disease, or symptoms like excessive thirst and frequent urination.

The second reason, and the more common one, is to monitor someone who already has diabetes. Rather than trying to remember every daily sugar reading, this test gives your doctor a clear sense of how well your sugar has been controlled over the past period, and helps them judge whether your current treatment is enough or needs adjusting.

What does your number mean?

The result is reported as a percentage, and the higher the number, the higher your average sugar has been. Broadly, medical guidelines around the world use two well-known cutoffs as reference points:

  • Normal range: a figure below 5.7% is usually considered within the normal range.
  • Prediabetes: a figure roughly between 5.7% and 6.4% may point to prediabetes, an important window to take notice and adjust your lifestyle.
  • Diabetes: a figure of 6.5% or above may indicate diabetes, which a doctor usually confirms by repeating the test or with other checks.

But note an important point: these numbers are general reference points, not a final verdict. Interpreting the result depends on your age, your health, your medicines, and the treatment goal your doctor sets, and the right target for one person can differ from another. So let your doctor explain what your own number means, and do not rush to judge it yourself.

What can skew the result?

The HbA1c test is accurate and useful in most cases, but it is not perfect in every situation. Because it relies on red blood cells and haemoglobin, any condition that affects these cells can push the number up or down, away from your true sugar level.

The main examples include:

  • Anaemia, iron deficiency, or a shortage of certain vitamins
  • Pregnancy, when the nature of the blood changes during this period
  • Some inherited blood disorders such as sickle cell anaemia and thalassaemia
  • Recent bleeding, a recent blood transfusion, or kidney disease

In such cases your doctor may rely on other tests alongside HbA1c, or interpret the result with more caution. This is another reason why reading the number alone, without a doctor, is not enough.

How often is it repeated?

There is no single rule that fits everyone. Someone whose sugar is stable and within their treatment goal may only repeat the test once or twice a year. Someone who has started a new treatment, changed their dose, or has sugar that is not well controlled may need it every few months to track progress.

Because the number reflects an average of several months, there is no point repeating it every week, as it does not change that quickly. Leave the timing to your doctor, who balances your condition against your treatment plan.

Everyday steps that help improve your number

The good news is that simple, consistent habits can show up in your number over time. You do not need drastic changes, just small steps you can keep up:

  • Cut back on added sugars, sweetened drinks, and desserts
  • Choose whole grains, vegetables, and legumes over refined starches
  • Move regularly, even a half-hour walk most days of the week
  • Keep a healthy weight, as losing even a little excess weight can make a difference
  • Aim for enough sleep and less stress, as both affect your sugar
  • If you take diabetes medication, stick to it exactly as your doctor prescribed

Remember these are general supportive steps and are not a substitute for the treatment plan your doctor sets for you.

When should you see a doctor?

See your doctor if you notice symptoms that can go with high blood sugar, such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, unexplained tiredness, or losing weight for no reason, or if you have risk factors for diabetes and want peace of mind. If you already have diabetes, discuss your latest result with your doctor rather than interpreting it on your own.

And remember, this article is for general awareness only and is not a substitute for medical advice. Only your doctor can connect your number to your full health picture and decide the next step.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to fast before an HbA1c test?

No. One of the advantages of this test is that it does not require fasting, because it measures your average blood sugar over the past few months rather than at that exact moment, so the sample can be taken at any time of day.

What is the difference between HbA1c and a fasting glucose test?

A fasting glucose test shows your sugar level only at the moment of the draw, after fasting, while HbA1c gives a picture of your average sugar over roughly the past two to three months. They complement each other, and your doctor decides which one you need.

How often should an HbA1c test be repeated?

It depends on your situation. Someone whose sugar is stable may repeat it once or twice a year, while someone adjusting treatment or with poor control may need it more often. Your doctor decides the timing that is right for you.

Can the HbA1c number be affected by things other than diabetes?

Yes. Some conditions such as anaemia, pregnancy, and inherited blood disorders can raise or lower the number away from your true sugar level, so your doctor interprets the result in the light of your full health and may order other tests to confirm.
