Beta hCG pregnancy blood test: how to read your number, when to repeat it, and when to go to the emergency department

In short

  • Ranges by week are extremely wide, so a single number cannot tell you whether a pregnancy is healthy.

  • Pain low down on one side, pain at the tip of the shoulder, or dizziness and fainting in early pregnancy are emergency signs that can mean an ectopic pregnancy.

What does your pregnancy blood test number mean?

A quantitative pregnancy blood test measures human chorionic gonadotropin, shown on your report as beta hCG. This hormone is made by the pregnancy cells that later form part of the placenta, so it only appears in meaningful amounts when there is a pregnancy. The result is usually given in mIU/mL, and some labs write IU/L instead. The two units are equal in value.

The simple rule used by the American Pregnancy Association is that a level under 5 mIU/mL is negative, meaning not pregnant, and a level over 25 mIU/mL is positive. Anything between 6 and 24 is a grey area, and the test is usually repeated two or three days later to see whether it rises. MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, also gives under 5 mIU/mL as the normal level in a woman who is not pregnant.

But one number does not answer the question most women really have: is the pregnancy healthy and in the right place? That answer comes from tracking the number over time, and then from an ultrasound once the level and the dates are high enough to see a pregnancy sac. This is why doctors so often ask for two tests 48 hours apart rather than one.

When should you test after a missed period?

MedlinePlus notes that hCG can appear in the blood and urine of a pregnant woman as early as 10 days after conception. A blood test is more sensitive than a urine test and can sometimes pick up a pregnancy a few days before your period is due, but a very early result can be low or even negative in a pregnancy that has only just started.

For most women, the most practical time to test is from the first day of a missed period onwards. If your cycle is irregular, as often happens with polycystic ovaries, count about two to three weeks from the last unprotected sex rather than relying on when your period should come. If the result is negative and your period still has not arrived, repeat the test after a few days to a week.

You do not need to fast before a beta hCG test, and it can be done at any time of day. A home urine test is best done on the first urine of the morning, because it is more concentrated. If you are having fertility treatment that includes an hCG trigger injection, that injection itself can stay in your blood for days and give a false positive, so stick to the test date your fertility doctor gives you.

Home urine test or blood test: which should you trust?

A home urine test from the pharmacy only detects whether the hormone is present. It shows two lines, or the words pregnant or not pregnant, but no number. It is quick, cheap and private, and in most cases it is enough to give you the first answer. Its accuracy depends on timing, how diluted your urine is from drinking lots of water, how you read the lines, and the quality of the strip itself, since some cheap brands sold in pharmacies are less sensitive than others.

A lab blood test gives an exact number, which is why it is called quantitative. Its real value shows when you need more than yes or no: when there is bleeding or pain, after a previous miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy, during fertility treatment, or when a urine result is faint and unclear. Some labs also run a qualitative blood test that only reports positive or negative, so ask for the quantitative one if your doctor wants to follow the trend.

A faint line on a urine test usually means the hormone is present at a low level, which can be a very early pregnancy. It is best to repeat the test in two days or to have a blood test. If a urine test is positive and your period then arrives a few days later, this may be what is called a chemical pregnancy, a pregnancy that stopped very early. It is common and usually does not mean there will be a problem next time.

hCG levels by week: why the ranges are so wide

Charts of hCG levels by week are everywhere online, and many women compare their number with them and panic. Look at the table below from MedlinePlus. At 5 weeks, for example, the range runs from 217 to 8,245 mIU/mL, so the top of the range is about 38 times the bottom. Two women at the same week with completely healthy pregnancies can have very different numbers.

The ranges are wide because weeks are usually counted from the first day of your last period, while the day of ovulation and conception varies between women and between cycles. The day the pregnancy implants in the womb also varies, and so does the machine used by the lab. If you ovulated a week late, your number will look low against the chart even though your pregnancy is perfectly healthy.

The hormone rises quickly in the first weeks, peaks roughly between weeks 8 and 10, then falls a little and levels off for the rest of the pregnancy. That is why hCG is not used to check on a pregnancy after about week 6 or 7, when ultrasound is far more informative. MedlinePlus also notes that values can vary slightly between laboratories.

Why one number is not enough: the 48-hour rule

In a healthy early pregnancy the hormone rises fast, and it is often said to double roughly every 48 hours. The real picture is more flexible. In a study published in Obstetrics and Gynecology in 2004, researchers at the University of Pennsylvania followed 287 women who came in with bleeding or pain and were later confirmed to have a healthy pregnancy in the womb, analysing 861 measurements. The median rise was 124 percent over two days.

The most important finding was that the slowest rise seen in a healthy pregnancy was 53 percent over two days, and 24 percent over one day. So a number that has not quite doubled after 48 hours does not necessarily mean the pregnancy is failing. For example, if your level was 400 and is 700 two days later, that is a rise of about 75 percent, which falls within what the researchers saw in healthy pregnancies.

On the other hand, a level that stays flat, rises very slowly or falls needs prompt assessment. Your doctor does not read the numbers alone but combines them with your symptoms and the ultrasound. Ask for both tests at the same lab, because differences between machines at two labs can create what looks like a rise or fall when there is none.

Low or slowly rising hCG: what can it mean?

The most common reason for a number that looks low is simply that the pregnancy is earlier than you think, because you ovulated later. This happens a lot with irregular cycles, or when you are not sure of the date of your last period. In that case the level rises normally on the second test, and the pregnancy shows on ultrasound later.

The second possibility is an early miscarriage, where the level stays flat or falls, often with bleeding and cramps. The third, and the most important for your safety, is an ectopic pregnancy, meaning the pregnancy has implanted outside the womb, usually in a fallopian tube. In an ectopic pregnancy the level often rises slowly or wobbles, and no pregnancy sac is seen inside the womb on ultrasound even though the level is high enough for one to be visible.

No single number separates these three possibilities, and you cannot tell them apart at home. At least two tests plus a transvaginal ultrasound are what make the picture clear. Until it is clear, do not start progesterone injections or any pregnancy support medicine on your own, even if the nearest pharmacy sells them without a prescription, because that decision belongs to the doctor once the pregnancy has been located.

Ectopic pregnancy warning signs that need emergency care

Ectopic pregnancy is not rare. In a ten-year study from King Fahd Hospital of the University in Al-Khobar, published in the Saudi Medical Journal in 2012, it occurred in 1.13 percent of cases. Abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding were the most common symptoms, and women who had IVF or ovulation induction, or a previous ectopic pregnancy, were more likely to have one.

The NHS says symptoms usually start between weeks 4 and 12 of pregnancy and include a missed period with other signs of pregnancy, pain low down on one side of the tummy, vaginal bleeding or a brown watery discharge, pain in the tip of the shoulder, and discomfort when passing urine or stool. Shoulder tip pain in particular is often ignored, yet it can be a sign of internal bleeding irritating the diaphragm.

An ectopic pregnancy can rupture and cause life-threatening internal bleeding. If you have the signs below and you are pregnant or might be, go to the nearest emergency department straight away. Do not wait for a second blood test or a clinic appointment. If you live far from a hospital, do not drive yourself; ask someone to take you.

Go to the emergency department now if, in early pregnancy, you have

  • Sharp, sudden and intense pain in your tummy
  • Ongoing pain low down on one side
  • Pain in the tip of your shoulder with no obvious cause
  • Feeling very dizzy or fainting
  • Looking very pale, or feeling sick with tummy pain
  • Heavy bleeding soaking a pad every hour

Very high hCG: twins or something else?

A number higher than expected for your week usually has a simple explanation: the pregnancy is further along than you calculated, because you ovulated earlier. Twins can also push the level up, but you cannot rely on the number to tell you about twins, since many single pregnancies produce high levels too. Ultrasound is what settles it.

A less common cause is a molar pregnancy, an abnormal growth of placental tissue that can push hCG very high, sometimes with bleeding or severe vomiting. The StatPearls review of hCG notes that measuring it also helps monitor women after a pregnancy loss and aids in diagnosing some rare tumours that produce the hormone. This is why a doctor may ask you to track the level until it returns to zero after a miscarriage or after treatment for a molar pregnancy.

Some positive results do not mean an ongoing pregnancy: hCG lingering for weeks after a recent miscarriage or birth, after an hCG injection during fertility treatment, and rarely interference in the lab machine itself. If your result does not fit your situation, explain this to your doctor and repeat the test rather than relying on one number.

Practical tips before and after the test

Repeat the test at the same lab whenever possible, keep every report with the date and time, and bring them to your doctor or the emergency department. Many women in the region test at a nearby lab and then see a doctor in another town, and organised reports save time and avoid repeat tests. Check that your report shows the unit, mIU/mL or IU/L.

If you have bleeding or pain, have had an ectopic pregnancy or pelvic surgery before, conceived after fertility treatment, or had a coil in place when you became pregnant, tell your doctor early, because these situations deserve closer follow-up. In a normal early pregnancy with no symptoms, calm follow-up with your doctor and an ultrasound at the right time is usually enough.

Remember that worrying about a number on a page is completely normal, and that most pregnancies that start with numbers that look alarming online turn out to be healthy. But symptoms come first: severe one-sided pain, shoulder tip pain or dizziness do not wait for another blood test. Frequently asked questions

What hCG level counts as a positive pregnancy test?

A level above 25 mIU/mL is usually considered positive, and below 5 mIU/mL negative. Results in between are a grey area, and the test is repeated two or three days later to see whether the level rises.

Can a blood test be negative if I am pregnant?

Yes, if it is done very early, before the hormone has risen enough. If your period is still late, repeat the test after a few days to a week, and see a doctor if you develop pain or bleeding.

Does hCG have to double every 48 hours?

In many healthy pregnancies it roughly doubles or more over two days, but a study of 287 women found the rise can be as little as 53 percent in a healthy pregnancy. Your doctor reads the rate of rise together with your symptoms and the ultrasound, not the number alone.

Do I need to fast before a pregnancy blood test?

No. A beta hCG test does not require fasting and can be done at any time of day. If other tests are being taken from the same sample, ask the lab about their instructions.

Does a very high hCG mean twins?

Twins can raise the level, but many single pregnancies also give high numbers, especially if the pregnancy is further along than you thought. Ultrasound is the only way to know how many babies there are.

When will the pregnancy sac show on ultrasound?

It usually appears once the pregnancy reaches a certain age and the hormone is high enough, and this varies between women and machines. If your level is high and no sac is seen in the womb, your doctor may arrange close follow-up to rule out an ectopic pregnancy.

Does hCG stay in the blood after a miscarriage?

Yes, it can stay for several weeks while it gradually falls, which is why a pregnancy test can still be positive after a miscarriage. Your doctor may track the level until it drops below 5 to confirm the miscarriage is complete.
