Levothyroxine: How to Take It So It Actually Works

By Adnan Alrefai · 14 July 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • Take levothyroxine on an empty stomach, at least 30 minutes before eating or drinking anything other than water.
  • Coffee, milk, calcium supplements and iron supplements significantly reduce its absorption if taken close together.
  • Do not expect an immediate change in how you feel. It takes weeks for thyroid hormone levels to stabilise.
  • TSH is re-tested six weeks after any dose change to see how well your body has responded.
  • Never adjust your dose yourself, even if you feel better or worse.

What is levothyroxine and why is it prescribed?

Levothyroxine is a synthetic copy of T4, the hormone the thyroid gland normally produces. It is prescribed when the thyroid does not make enough hormone on its own, whether because of an autoimmune condition such as Hashimoto's thyroiditis, because the gland has been surgically removed, or because it was treated with radioactive iodine.

The medicine replaces what the thyroid is no longer making and restores the body's hormonal balance. Once T4 reaches the tissues, some of it converts to the more active T3, which directly influences metabolism, energy, heart rate and mood.

Levothyroxine is chemically straightforward but behaviourally sensitive. A small change in when you take it, or what you eat and drink nearby, can meaningfully alter how much reaches your bloodstream.

When should you take it and how does food affect absorption?

The small intestine absorbs levothyroxine, and that absorption is heavily influenced by what is already in the stomach. The standard recommendation is to take it first thing in the morning on a completely empty stomach, at least 30 minutes before eating or drinking, with some guidelines advising a full hour for optimal absorption.

A 2014 review published in the European Review for Medical and Pharmacological Sciences found that food in the stomach can reduce levothyroxine absorption by 40 to 80 percent compared with taking it fasted. That means someone who consistently swallows the tablet with breakfast may need a substantially higher dose to achieve the same effect.

If taking it in the morning is difficult because of shift work, fasting, or a busy routine, some studies suggest that taking it at bedtime, at least two hours after the last meal, can produce comparable results in certain patients. Discuss this option with your doctor before switching.

What blocks its absorption?

This is what surprises most patients. Regular coffee reduces levothyroxine absorption significantly, even with a 15-minute gap. Researchers writing in Best Practice and Research in 2009 documented that coffee delays and reduces absorption, leading to lower circulating T4 levels. Strong tea carries a similar risk because of its tannin content.

Calcium supplements and iron supplements bind to levothyroxine in the gut and prevent it from crossing into the bloodstream. The guidance is to leave at least four hours between levothyroxine and these supplements. Milk and dairy products are rich in calcium and carry the same risk, which is why even a milky coffee within an hour can matter.

Acid-suppressing medicines, certain cholesterol-lowering drugs from the bile acid sequestrant class, raloxifene, and some proton pump inhibitors are all documented as interfering with absorption. Tell every prescribing doctor you see that you take levothyroxine, so interactions can be anticipated.

Why does your dose change over time?

Your dose is not fixed for life because your body's need for thyroid hormone changes with circumstances. A 2019 review in Advances in Therapy documented that several situations call for dose review: significant weight change, pregnancy, advancing age, starting a new medicine that affects absorption, or switching between brand-name and generic formulations.

Pregnancy raises the body's demand for T4 noticeably during the first trimester. A woman already taking levothyroxine typically needs a higher dose from early pregnancy, and TSH monitoring becomes more frequent than usual. Tell your doctor as soon as you know you are pregnant.

Ageing and the menopause can warrant a lower dose because metabolism slows. Bariatric surgery and other procedures that affect the gut may change absorption entirely. Regular follow-up is not a formality but the only reliable way to confirm the dose is still right for you.

When is TSH re-tested after starting the medicine?

Levothyroxine does not raise thyroid hormones overnight. The body needs weeks for T4 to accumulate and stabilise in the blood, and the pituitary gland's TSH response adjusts even more slowly behind that.

The accepted recommendation is to recheck TSH six weeks after starting the dose or after any change. A common mistake is testing too early and interpreting an unstable result, or waiting many months without any check at all.

Once TSH settles within the target range and symptoms improve, monitoring every six to twelve months is typically enough. Any new medicine, significant weight change or return of symptoms warrants an earlier check.

What if you miss a dose?

Because levothyroxine accumulates slowly, missing a single dose does not cause an immediate crisis. If you remember the same day, take it as soon as you can on an empty stomach. If you only remember the next morning, skip the missed dose and return to your normal schedule.

Do not take two doses in one day to compensate. A doubled dose can trigger symptoms that mimic an overactive thyroid, including palpitations, anxiety and difficulty sleeping.

If missed doses are becoming frequent because of a busy schedule, placing the tablet somewhere you will see it first thing in the morning, or using a daily reminder on your phone, makes a measurable difference to consistency.

Side effects worth knowing about

Most people who take the correct dose notice no side effects. Problems arise when the dose is higher than the body needs, producing symptoms that resemble an overactive thyroid: a racing or irregular heartbeat, unusual anxiety or restlessness, excessive sweating, unintended weight loss, or disturbed sleep.

On the other side, if the dose is too low, the symptoms of an underactive thyroid such as fatigue, feeling cold, constipation and weight gain may persist. This does not mean the medicine is not working but often means the dose needs adjustment after TSH is checked.

In rare cases, a person reacts to one of the inactive ingredients in a particular tablet rather than to levothyroxine itself. Switching brand or formulation can resolve this, but the decision belongs to the prescribing doctor.

Contact your doctor promptly if you notice

  • A fast or irregular heartbeat after a dose change
  • Chest pain or breathlessness
  • Swelling of the face, lips or tongue
  • Concerning new symptoms that appeared after switching brand or formulation

Generic versus branded levothyroxine: does it matter?

In many countries, levothyroxine is available under several brand names as well as generic versions. The active ingredient is chemically identical, but the inactive fillers and binders in the tablet can differ between manufacturers, and these differences can affect how quickly the tablet dissolves and how much is absorbed.

The established international recommendation is to avoid switching between formulations without telling your doctor, because a change may require a dose adjustment. If you live in a country where your usual brand is unavailable or has become unaffordable, tell the prescribing doctor before switching so that a TSH recheck can be planned six weeks after the change.

In parts of the Middle East and North Africa, levothyroxine is sometimes dispensed without a prescription or on an old prescription that has not been reviewed. This is tempting but genuinely risky. Hashimoto's disease progresses gradually, so the dose that was correct two years ago may be too low today, and a person can continue suffering fatigue and weight changes without realising the dose is the problem.

Taking it consistently is the real medicine

Levothyroxine taken consistently and correctly is a fundamentally different treatment from the same tablet taken inconsistently or with food. A randomised clinical trial published in 2026 in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism compared fasting and non-fasting administration and found that equivalent TSH outcomes were achievable with dose adjustment, but that consistency in whichever method was chosen remained the most important single factor.

The practical conclusion is to choose a routine you can maintain every day, whether that is first thing in the morning or at bedtime, and tell your doctor exactly what that routine is so results can be interpreted against what you are actually doing. A patient who describes their real behaviour gives the doctor far more useful information than one who describes the ideal behaviour they wish they were following.

Do not stop the medicine on your own even if you feel completely well. With conditions like Hashimoto's thyroiditis, the gland continues to need support even when you are asymptomatic. Only a doctor, reviewing your TSH and free T4 alongside your current symptoms, should decide whether the dose can be reduced or stopped. Stopping abruptly can see symptoms return gradually over several weeks before you realise the decision was a mistake.

Questions specific to women

Women are more susceptible to thyroid conditions generally, and several life stages intersect with the need for careful monitoring. Pregnancy is the most significant, as discussed. The postpartum period can bring a transient autoimmune thyroiditis that temporarily alters the dose requirement, sometimes pushing it higher and sometimes, as the inflammation resolves, allowing it to be reduced again.

The menopause brings metabolic changes that may reduce the dose needed. Women who start calcium supplements or bisphosphonates for bone health should tell the doctor managing their thyroid, because the interaction is real and may require a timing strategy or dose review. Some women are surprised to notice fatigue and weight gain returning even though they have not changed their levothyroxine dose, and the explanation turns out to be a new calcium supplement taken at the wrong time.

Hormonal contraceptives and oestrogen replacement therapy can raise the level of the protein that carries thyroid hormone in the blood, potentially requiring a higher levothyroxine dose. Report all hormonal medicines to every doctor who prescribes for you, and if you start or stop hormonal contraception, a TSH check at the next scheduled visit is worthwhile.

Some women in the region are reluctant to continue levothyroxine through pregnancy out of concern about the effect on the baby. That concern is misplaced. Levothyroxine does not cross the placenta in significant amounts. What does carry real risk for fetal neurological development is undertreated hypothyroidism during pregnancy. The medicine is not the danger; stopping it is.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take levothyroxine with tea instead of coffee?

Strong tea contains tannins that reduce levothyroxine absorption in a similar way to coffee. It is best to wait 30 to 60 minutes before drinking either. Plain water is the only drink that can safely accompany the tablet.

My TSH is still high even though I take the medicine every day. Why?

The most common reasons are taking the tablet with food or coffee, a drug interaction such as calcium or iron taken too close, or a dose that is simply lower than your body currently needs. Tell your doctor precisely when and how you take the medicine to help them identify the cause.

Will I need levothyroxine for the rest of my life?

In most cases of permanent hypothyroidism, such as Hashimoto's disease or a removed thyroid gland, the medicine is lifelong. Some temporary conditions, such as postpartum thyroiditis, may resolve and allow the medicine to be stopped later. Your doctor decides based on your specific diagnosis.

Can I switch brands on my own?

It is better not to switch without telling your doctor, because absorption can differ between formulations even at the same written dose. If you have to change because of availability, let your doctor know and recheck TSH after six weeks.

Is levothyroxine safe while breastfeeding?

Yes. The amount that passes into breast milk is very small and is not considered harmful to an infant. What matters most is that the mother maintains a normal hormone level for her own health and wellbeing.

How do I manage levothyroxine during Ramadan fasting?

Take it at suhoor time on an empty stomach, then wait 30 to 60 minutes before eating the suhoor meal. Some doctors recommend taking it two hours after the iftar meal before sleep. Discuss the best approach for your routine with your doctor before Ramadan begins.

How do I know my dose is right?

The only reliable way is to measure TSH and often free T4, as your doctor advises. Symptoms matter but are not enough on their own, because some people continue to feel unwell even with a normal TSH and need a broader assessment.

Does levothyroxine cause osteoporosis?

Doses that keep TSH suppressed well below the normal range over many years are associated with increased bone loss, particularly in postmenopausal women. This is why the target is to keep TSH within the range appropriate for your condition, not to push it lower than necessary.
