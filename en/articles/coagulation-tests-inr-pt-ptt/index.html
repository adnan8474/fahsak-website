PT, INR and aPTT: how to read your blood clotting results

By Adnan Alrefai · 14 July 2026 · 7 min read

In short

  • PT measures how quickly blood clots via the extrinsic pathway, and INR is an internationally standardised version of that result so different labs give comparable numbers.
  • aPTT measures the intrinsic clotting pathway and is mainly used to monitor heparin doses in hospital.
  • For warfarin patients, the INR target depends on the reason: 2.0 to 3.0 for most conditions, and 2.5 to 3.5 for mechanical heart valves.
  • Foods rich in vitamin K, such as leafy vegetables, and many common medicines can raise or lower INR sharply.
  • Unusual bleeding or a very high INR requires urgent medical attention, not a wait-and-see approach.

What do clotting tests actually measure?

When you are injured, your body triggers a precise chain of chemical reactions called the coagulation cascade. This cascade runs through two main pathways that converge to form a clot and stop bleeding. Clotting tests measure the efficiency of this system and identify whether any component is not working correctly.

PT, or Prothrombin Time, measures how quickly the extrinsic pathway of clotting works. This pathway includes the factors that depend on vitamin K, and these are the same factors that warfarin acts on. The aPTT, or activated Partial Thromboplastin Time, measures the intrinsic pathway, which is more sensitive to unfractionated heparin given intravenously in hospital.

INR, or International Normalized Ratio, is not a separate test. It is a calculation that standardises the PT result across different laboratories. A study published in 2021 in the International Journal of Laboratory Hematology noted that before INR was introduced, PT results varied significantly from lab to lab depending on the reagent used. INR solved that problem, which is why an INR of 2.5 in Riyadh means the same as an INR of 2.5 in Cairo or Amman.

What is a normal result for each test?

A normal PT is roughly 11 to 14 seconds in most laboratories, but this figure varies by reagent, so always read your result against the range printed on your own report rather than a number you read elsewhere. The normal INR for a person not taking anticoagulants is between 0.9 and 1.1, very close to 1.

A normal aPTT is usually 25 to 35 seconds. Its importance lies largely in monitoring unfractionated heparin given by intravenous drip in hospital during surgery or for acute clot treatment. It also reveals deficiencies in certain clotting factors in cases of unexplained bleeding.

A PT or aPTT that is longer than normal means blood is taking more time than usual to clot, which raises the risk of bleeding. A result shorter than normal is rarely clinically important in most situations, but any result outside the reference range warrants a conversation with your doctor.

Why do some people take warfarin, and what is the INR target?

Warfarin is an anticoagulant medicine used to prevent blood clots in people at increased risk of forming them. It is prescribed for conditions including atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, and mechanical prosthetic heart valves. Warfarin remains a commonly used anticoagulant across the Arab world because of its availability and relatively low cost compared with newer agents.

The INR target for warfarin patients depends on why they are taking it. For most conditions such as atrial fibrillation and venous clots, the target is between 2.0 and 3.0. For mechanical heart valves the target is higher, between 2.5 and 3.5. Do not try to determine your own target from a table. Your doctor sets the exact range for your specific situation.

Keeping INR within the therapeutic range is delicate because the margin between clotting risk and bleeding risk is narrow. An INR below the target means the medicine is not doing enough and a clot may form. An INR above the target means the bleeding risk has risen. A 2011 study in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology confirmed that the proportion of time a patient's INR stays within their therapeutic range directly affects their safety from both clots and bleeding.

What makes INR swing up or down?

Vitamin K is essential for the synthesis of several clotting factors, and warfarin works specifically by blocking it. The amount of vitamin K you eat directly affects how well warfarin works and therefore your INR reading. A systematic review published in Medicine in 2016 confirmed that sudden changes in consumption of vitamin K-rich leafy vegetables are among the most common reasons for INR falling outside its target range.

Many medicines interact with warfarin. Antibiotics, particularly metronidazole, co-trimoxazole, and fluoroquinolones, raise INR noticeably. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen increase the bleeding risk. Certain herbal products, especially ginkgo, ginseng, and garlic in large amounts, can also interfere. Always tell your doctor and pharmacist every medicine you are taking before starting anything new.

Other factors that influence INR include: acute illness, especially liver conditions, severe diarrhoea, large changes in weight, and some foods and drinks including grapefruit juice. Regular monitoring at the intervals your doctor sets is the only reliable way to catch these changes early.

When does a high PT or INR point to a liver problem?

The liver is the main factory for most clotting factors, particularly factors II, VII, IX, and X, which all depend on vitamin K for their activation. When the liver is severely diseased, as in cirrhosis or serious acute hepatitis or acute liver failure, it produces fewer of these factors, and PT and INR rise even in someone taking no anticoagulant medicine at all. This rise differs from warfarin-related elevation because it does not fully correct when vitamin K is given.

This is why liver specialists use PT and INR as key markers of liver function and the degree of damage. They form part of scoring systems used to classify patients with chronic liver disease and to prioritise liver transplantation. A gradually rising INR in someone whose warfarin dose has not changed is good reason to reassess liver function fully. A patient with advanced chronic hepatitis B or C may notice a slow upward drift in their INR over the years that reflects accumulating fibrosis.

Liver disease is not the only non-warfarin cause of a raised PT or aPTT. Inherited clotting factor deficiencies such as haemophilia prolong aPTT while leaving PT normal, because the missing factors belong to the intrinsic pathway rather than the extrinsic one. Dietary vitamin K deficiency or malabsorption prolongs PT because those factors cannot be properly activated. In disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), both PT and aPTT rise together as the clotting system is consumed in an uncontrolled fashion. In all these cases, the doctor interprets the result in the context of your complete medical history, symptoms, and all other test results.

How do you prepare correctly for a PT or INR test?

In most cases, fasting is not required before a PT, INR, or aPTT test. These tests measure protein activity in the blood, not glucose or lipid levels that are affected by eating. Tell the laboratory that you are on warfarin when you arrive, so the sample is handled correctly.

The best time to have an INR drawn is just before your next warfarin dose, not immediately after taking it, because this reflects the most stable drug level. Try to have the test under similar conditions each time, meaning a comparable diet over the preceding days.

A 2021 study from Jordan published in the International Journal of Medical Sciences found that genetic differences in warfarin metabolism are common in Arab populations. This means one person may need a very different dose from another person with the same condition, which is an additional reason why regular monitoring matters.

What warning signs mean you need emergency care?

Anyone on an anticoagulant medicine needs to recognise abnormal bleeding promptly, because it can escalate quickly. Minor bruising or an occasional nosebleed is expected on these medicines, but certain signs require going to an emergency department without delay. The most dangerous type of bleeding for a person on anticoagulants is intracranial bleeding, which is uncommon but possible, especially in older patients or those with uncontrolled high blood pressure. A fall or a knock to the head can trigger this even when no external wound is visible.

When INR reaches very high numbers even without visible bleeding, call your doctor or go to the emergency department. The risk is present before symptoms appear. An INR above 5.0 on warfarin calls for immediate medical review. A 2010 paper in the Journal of Thrombosis and Thrombolysis emphasised the importance of rapid response to excessive INR elevation to prevent bleeding before it occurs. In emergency settings, doctors can reverse warfarin quickly using vitamin K and clotting factor concentrates if needed.

Go to emergency immediately if you notice

  • Bleeding that does not stop with direct pressure after 10 minutes
  • Black or very dark tarry stools, which may signal stomach or gut bleeding
  • Blood in the urine, red or dark brown
  • Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
  • Sudden severe headache or weakness on one side of the body, which may signal a brain bleed
  • A fall or blow to the head while on anticoagulants, even if you feel fine afterwards

Is an aPTT test enough on its own to diagnose a clotting disorder?

No single test is sufficient to diagnose a clotting disorder. A doctor typically orders several tests together: PT, aPTT, platelet count, and sometimes fibrinogen, D-dimer, and specific clotting factor levels. It is the pattern across these tests combined that guides the diagnosis, not any single number in isolation.

For example, a raised aPTT with a normal PT prompts a search for a deficiency in factor VIII, IX, or XI (the haemophilia factors), or the presence of what is called a lupus anticoagulant, which despite its name is not only found in lupus patients. Both PT and aPTT raised together may point to liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), in which the clotting system is consumed by runaway activation. A falling platelet count alongside rising D-dimer and prolonged both tests strongly suggests DIC and demands urgent investigation.

Your doctor interprets this pattern in the light of your symptoms, your medical history, and the rest of your results. The result sheet alone does not tell the whole story. It provides the data that the doctor uses to build the complete picture. If you have an abnormal result in any of these tests and you are not taking an anticoagulant, do not dismiss it. Ask your doctor what it means and whether any further testing is needed to understand whether your clotting system is functioning correctly.

Frequently asked questions

Does a high INR always mean I am bleeding?

No. A high INR means your blood is clotting more slowly than normal, which raises the risk of bleeding, but actual bleeding may not be happening at that moment. Even so, a very high INR needs prompt medical attention because the risk is real before any symptoms appear.

Are spinach and leafy greens banned on warfarin?

They are not banned, but their quantity needs to stay consistent from week to week. The problem is a sudden large change in vitamin K intake, not eating leafy greens regularly. Talk to your doctor or a dietitian about amounts that fit your usual eating pattern.

How do I know whether my INR is stable?

A stable INR is one that stays within your therapeutic target range for most readings over several months. If your results swing widely from one test to the next, your doctor will need to review your dose and look at the factors causing the variation.

Do I need to fast before an INR test?

No. This test is not affected by eating. However, try to keep your diet consistent in the days before the test, because large changes in dietary vitamin K can nudge the result.

What does a raised aPTT with a normal PT mean?

This pattern calls for medical assessment. It may point to a deficiency in a specific clotting factor or the presence of what is called a lupus anticoagulant. Your doctor will decide whether further tests are needed to identify the cause.

Can I take painkillers while on warfarin?

Paracetamol at usual doses is the safest painkiller for most warfarin patients, though high or prolonged doses can nudge INR upwards. Anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen and aspirin increase the risk of bleeding and should be avoided without your doctor's advice.

Is aPTT used to monitor heparin at home?

No. Unfractionated heparin monitored by aPTT is given only by intravenous drip in hospital. At home, patients use either warfarin monitored by INR, or low-molecular-weight heparin injected under the skin, which does not generally require regular blood tests.
