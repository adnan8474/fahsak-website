Water quality and waterborne disease: practical protection for your household

By Adnan Alrefai · 4 August 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Boiling for one full minute kills virtually all bacteria, viruses and parasites that cause waterborne disease, making it the most reliable home treatment.
  • Intermittent piped water supply significantly increases contamination risk because low pressure allows pollutants to be drawn into pipes through cracks and joints.
  • Your household water tank needs cleaning at least every three months, or it becomes a contamination source in its own right.
  • Severe diarrhoea with blood or mucus, or with signs of dehydration, is a medical emergency that cannot be managed at home with fluids alone.
  • Chlorine kills most bacteria but does not reliably eliminate hard-walled parasites such as Cryptosporidium, which is why boiling or fine filtration is needed for full coverage.

How does safe water become dangerous?

Water that leaves a treatment plant clean does not necessarily arrive at your tap in the same condition. A study published in Water Research in 2013 compared microbial water quality in intermittent and continuous piped supply systems and found that intermittent supply carried substantially higher bacterial contamination. When water stops flowing and pressure in the pipes drops, contaminated water from surrounding soil or nearby sewage can be drawn in through any crack, joint or corroded section of pipework.

Household storage tanks create a second hazard. In a tank that is not cleaned regularly, algae, bacteria and a bacterial film known as biofilm accumulate gradually on the interior walls and contaminate water held there even if the incoming supply was clean. In hot climates, certain bacteria multiply rapidly in warm standing water. Any crack in a tank lid or its connecting pipes opens a route for contamination from air, insects or debris.

Areas where infrastructure has been disrupted by conflict or natural disaster face elevated risk. A review published in Frontiers in Public Health in 2023 documented the cholera outbreak in Syria as directly linked to the collapse of water supply and sanitation infrastructure, a pattern seen repeatedly across the region during crises. A case-control study of drinking water quality in Yemen, published in 2024, confirmed that households using unimproved water sources faced measurably higher risk of illness.

Boiling: the simplest and most reliable method

Boiling is the oldest and most reliably effective method for household water treatment. Temperatures above 70 degrees Celsius kill most pathogens, and a full rolling boil at sea level kills all common bacteria, viruses and parasites including Cryptosporidium and Giardia, both of which resist chlorine. At one minute of full boil, the water is safe. In higher altitude areas such as mountain regions, boil for three minutes because the boiling point decreases with altitude and pathogen kill takes longer.

Boiling does not remove chemical contamination or heavy metals such as lead and arsenic, and it does not improve taste caused by dissolved organic compounds. It is the right solution for microbial and parasitic contamination, which is the primary concern in most regional settings. After boiling, cool the water in a clean closed container and do not add any unboiled water to it, whether from a bottle or a tap.

The container you use for cooling and storing boiled water matters as much as the boiling itself. A metal or glass vessel with a secure lid prevents recontamination during the cooling and storage period. Avoid wide-mouthed plastic containers or any vessel that is not thoroughly clean. Contamination introduced during storage after boiling can negate the protection that the boiling provided.

Household chlorine: how to use it correctly

Chlorine is the most widely used method for treating larger quantities of water at very low cost. It is typically applied as sodium hypochlorite solution, which is the active ingredient in household bleach products available in most pharmacies and general stores. A landmark study published in Water Science and Technology in 2003 confirmed the effectiveness of household chlorine treatment in reducing bacterial contamination and diarrhoeal illness in settings comparable to those across the region.

The standard dose is generally two drops of a 5 to 6 percent sodium hypochlorite solution per litre of water. Stir well and wait at least 30 minutes before drinking. For turbid or visibly cloudy water, double the dose because organic matter in the water consumes chlorine and reduces its effectiveness. The treated water should have a faint smell of chlorine. If you cannot detect any chlorine odour after 30 minutes, add a little more and wait again.

Chlorine does not reliably kill Cryptosporidium or Giardia at safe household concentrations. If your area is experiencing an outbreak linked to either of these parasites, boiling is the required solution, not chlorination alone. Chlorine also loses effectiveness rapidly when exposed to sunlight and heat, so store your chlorine solution in a cool place away from direct light, and use it before its expiry date.

Household filters: which ones actually work?

Home water filters fall into two broad types: chemical filters that use chlorine or iodine to disinfect, and mechanical filters that physically block particles and microorganisms. A systematic review published in the Journal of Global Health in 2024 confirmed that fine-pore mechanical filters, specifically the hollow fibre type used in products such as the LifeStraw Family filter, produce a meaningful and measurable reduction in childhood diarrhoea when used consistently.

A hollow fibre filter with a pore size of 0.1 micrometres or smaller is the most effective mechanical option. At that scale it intercepts bacteria and parasites reliably. Activated carbon filters improve taste and odour and remove some chemical contaminants but do not disinfect water against bacteria. Combining a fine mechanical filter with chlorine treatment delivers broader protection than either method alone.

Every filter requires maintenance or it becomes a contamination source itself. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for replacing filter elements and cleaning the housing. A filter that has exceeded its rated operational life may release accumulated bacteria rather than retaining them. Note the installation date and change elements on schedule.

Keeping your water tank clean

Roof and ground-level household water storage tanks need to be cleaned at least every three months in hot climates, and every six months in cooler areas. Calcium carbonate deposits, algae and a bacterial layer known as biofilm accumulate gradually on the tank walls and contaminate the stored water even when the incoming supply is clean.

To clean the tank: empty it completely, then scrub the interior walls with a stiff brush or rough cloth without strongly scented chemical cleaners. Rinse thoroughly with water. Then prepare a chlorine solution using one litre of standard 5 percent bleach per thousand litres of tank capacity. Fill the tank with this solution and leave it for two hours before drinking. If the chlorine odour is strong after two hours, flush the tank once with clean water before use.

Check the tank lid. A damaged or poorly fitting lid allows insects, birds, dust and debris to enter, all of which are contamination routes. Never reach into the tank with a hand or dip a container directly into the water. Use a tap or small pump to draw water out. A hand introduced into the tank contaminates the water for everyone drawing from it.

Signs that your water tank may be contaminated

  • Unpleasant smell or unusual taste in tap water
  • Visible green, brown or grey colour in the water
  • Visible sediment or a film on the water surface
  • Insects or small animals found inside the tank
  • Repeated unexplained diarrhoea among household members

Warning signs and when to see a doctor

Mild diarrhoea lasting one or two days in a healthy adult is usually managed at home with increased fluids and oral rehydration solution. But certain patterns require urgent medical assessment. Very profuse watery diarrhoea that makes it impossible to keep up with fluid intake, diarrhoea containing blood or mucus, fever above 39 degrees Celsius alongside diarrhoea, and any diarrhoea in a child under two years old all warrant medical evaluation.

Children under two are a specific priority because dehydration develops faster in young children and can become life-threatening within hours. The warning signs of severe dehydration are: no tears when crying, dry mouth and tongue, skin that does not spring back quickly when pinched, sunken eyes, very little or no urine for several hours, and lethargy or unusual drowsiness. Any of these in a young child is an emergency. Start oral rehydration solution immediately and get to a hospital.

An adult who develops a fever that rises gradually over several days, accompanied by headache and body aches, without another clear cause may have typhoid fever or hepatitis A, both of which spread through contaminated water and food and both of which need laboratory confirmation and proper medical treatment. These are not conditions to manage at home with available medicines.

Go to a doctor immediately if you notice

  • Profuse watery diarrhoea with inability to drink enough to keep up (the main sign of cholera)
  • Blood or mucus in the stool with fever
  • Signs of severe dehydration: dry mouth, no urine, dizziness or confusion
  • Fever above 39 degrees Celsius lasting more than 72 hours
  • Any severe diarrhoea in a child under two, an elderly person or someone with a chronic illness

Everyday prevention habits that genuinely reduce risk

Washing hands with soap before eating and after using the toilet is the single cheapest and most effective measure against waterborne and foodborne illness. The large Lancet meta-analysis published in 2022, reviewing interventions in low-income and middle-income settings, found that improvements in water quality combined with soap handwashing reduced childhood diarrhoea by up to 50 percent. The soap is not optional. Water alone removes far fewer pathogens than water with soap.

Do not drink from irrigation canals, rivers or ponds without treatment. These open water sources are common routes for Giardia, Cryptosporidium, Vibrio cholerae and typhoid bacteria in areas where sewage and water supplies are not fully separated. Vegetables irrigated with untreated water should be washed thoroughly in clean water or soaked briefly in chlorinated water before consumption.

When travelling to areas where you are unsure of water quality, factory-sealed bottles are the safest option. If those are not available, boiled water is the next best choice. Avoid ice in drinks at restaurants and street food stalls unless you are confident in its source, because ice made from untreated water carries the same risk as the water itself.

Hand hygiene at critical moments reduces disease transmission even when the water itself is not perfectly treated. The moments that matter most are before preparing or eating food, after using the toilet, after handling animals or soil, and after caring for someone who is ill. Keep a small bar of soap accessible at every hand-washing point in the house, because the habit only forms when the soap is already there.

When oral rehydration solution (ORS) is not commercially available, you can prepare a reliable home version by dissolving one level teaspoon of salt and six level teaspoons of sugar in one litre of cooled boiled water, with a squeeze of lemon juice if available. This replaces the salt and glucose lost through diarrhoea and prevents the dehydration that turns a manageable illness into a dangerous one. Carbonated drinks and concentrated fruit juices are not substitutes because their high sugar concentration can draw more fluid from the gut and worsen the situation.

Frequently asked questions

Is one minute of boiling really enough to make water safe?

Yes. A full rolling boil for one minute at sea level kills all common bacteria, viruses and parasites including those that resist chlorine. At altitudes above 2,000 metres, boil for three minutes. After boiling, store the water in a clean covered container.

Does household chlorine kill all pathogens?

Chlorine is highly effective against bacteria and most viruses, but it does not reliably kill Cryptosporidium at safe household doses. If your area has a Cryptosporidium-related outbreak, boiling or a fine hollow fibre filter is required in addition to or instead of chlorination.

How often should a household water tank be cleaned?

At least every three months in hot climates. If you see visible algae growth, a green colour, unusual odour or taste, clean the tank immediately regardless of when it was last done. A contaminated tank contaminates all the water drawn from it.

Is tap water in Syrian or Iraqi cities safe to drink?

This varies by area and time. Boiling is the safest default if you are unsure. During or after periods of infrastructure disruption or intermittent supply, treat all tap water as potentially unsafe without treatment before drinking.

Does a household filter replace boiling?

A good hollow fibre filter with pore size 0.1 micrometres or smaller removes bacteria and parasites reliably but does not kill viruses at the same level as boiling. Combining a fine filter with chlorination covers a broader range of pathogens. If neither is available, boiling remains the most reliable and cheapest option.

How do I make water safe for an infant?

For infants under one year, boiling is always the recommended method because their immune systems are still developing. For older children, properly chlorinated or filtered water is acceptable. Do not give a turbid or suspect water source to an infant even after chemical treatment. When in doubt, boil.

Is ice at restaurants and food stalls safe?

Ice is made from water, so if the water is untreated the ice carries the same risk. In areas where you are not certain of water source safety, avoid ice in drinks and ask for beverages in sealed factory bottles.

Can diarrhoea be treated at home?

Mild to moderate diarrhoea in otherwise healthy adults can be managed at home with rest, increased fluids and oral rehydration solution (ORS). Seek medical attention for diarrhoea with blood or mucus, high fever, signs of dehydration, or any diarrhoea in a child under two, an elderly person or someone with chronic illness.
