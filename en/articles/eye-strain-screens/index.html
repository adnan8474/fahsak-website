Eye Strain from Screens: Causes, Relief and What It Means for Your Children's Sight

By Adnan Alrefai · 14 July 2026 · 8 min read

In short

  • Staring at a screen for hours cuts your blink rate by half, drying the eye surface and causing tiredness, headaches and blurred vision.
  • The 20-20-20 rule works: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
  • Children who spend two or more hours a day outdoors in natural daylight have slower myopia progression.
  • Good room lighting, correct screen distance and regular breaks fix most day-to-day digital eye strain.
  • If your child squints, holds books very close, or complains of headaches after reading, book a sight test now.

What is digital eye strain?

Digital eye strain, also called computer vision syndrome, is a cluster of symptoms that build up during extended use of screens, whether a phone, computer, tablet or television. The symptoms include heaviness or burning in the eyes, a dull headache across the forehead, blurred vision that comes and goes during or after screen work, and aching in the neck and shoulders.

These symptoms do not mean your eyes are damaged. Think of it like muscle fatigue: the eye muscles are held in one position for too long without rest, and they protest. A 2022 review published in Ophthalmology and Therapy, titled 'Digital Eye Strain: A Comprehensive Review' by Kaur, Gurnani and colleagues, confirmed that digital eye strain has become one of the most common visual complaints globally, with rates rising sharply alongside screen hours.

The important distinction is between transient discomfort, which is very common and manageable, and persistent symptoms that point to an underlying refractive problem. If your vision is blurry even when you are not at a screen, that is worth investigating rather than waiting.

Symptoms of digital eye strain

  • Burning or itching eyes after screen use
  • Blurred or double vision mid-session
  • Headache across the brow or temples
  • Neck and shoulder pain
  • Increased light sensitivity
  • Difficulty refocusing after long screen sessions

Why do your eyes get tired in front of a screen?

The main reason is reduced blinking. Under normal conditions, a person blinks about 15 times per minute. That rate can halve when you are focused on a screen, leaving the eye surface exposed and drying out. Research published in Acta Ophthalmologica in 2022, titled 'Video display terminal use and dry eye' by Kamoy and colleagues, confirmed a direct link between prolonged screen work and dry eye symptoms driven by this reduced blink rate.

A second factor is sustained near focus. The small ciliary muscles inside the eye hold the lens in a contracted shape when you look at something close. Holding that contraction for hours without release causes the same fatigue as holding your arm out straight for an extended period. Add to that glare from the screen surface, reflections from windows behind you, and a brightness contrast between the bright screen and a dim room, and you have several stresses combining.

Screen quality matters too. Older monitors with low refresh rates or low resolution force the visual system to work harder to build a stable image. Correct screen distance, roughly 50 to 70 centimetres for a desk monitor, is one of the simplest fixes.

Does the 20-20-20 rule actually work?

The 20-20-20 rule asks you to look away from your screen every 20 minutes at an object at least 20 feet (about 6 metres) away for 20 seconds. That distance is enough for the ciliary muscles to fully relax from their near-focus contraction, which is what provides the relief.

A 2023 study published in Contact Lens and Anterior Eye, titled 'The effects of breaks on digital eye strain, dry eye and binocular vision: Testing the 20-20-20 rule' by Talens-Estarelles and colleagues, showed that regular breaks during screen work measurably reduced both dry eye symptoms and visual discomfort compared to continuous work without breaks. The rule does not correct an existing prescription problem, but it does meaningfully reduce the daily fatigue load.

In practice, the easiest way to apply it is to set a phone timer or use a screen break app. Any natural pause, finishing a task, getting up for water, even a brief conversation, can double as your 20-second break if you use it to look across the room or out a window.

Myopia in children: is it the screens or something else?

Myopia, the inability to see distant objects clearly, is increasing rapidly in children worldwide, including in the Arab region. Screen time is part of the picture, but the evidence points to a more specific cause: lack of time outdoors in natural light.

A 2026 study published in PloS One, titled 'Effect of screen time and outdoor activities on myopia progression' by Zougheib, Salameh and colleagues, found that greater time spent outdoors was associated with slower progression of myopia. Natural daylight is bright at an intensity that indoor lighting, including screens, simply cannot match, and that intensity appears to stimulate a dopamine-related protective mechanism in the developing retina that slows excessive eye growth.

The practical message for families is that the most effective single action to protect your child's distance vision is ensuring two or more hours of time outside in daylight every day. That is more protective than banning screens outright, though reducing prolonged near work is also sensible.

Dry eye: when do you need lubricating drops?

Dry eye is among the most common screen-related complaints. You may feel it as a gritty sensation, burning, or vision that briefly blurs and then clears when you blink. Artificial tear drops, sold without prescription at any pharmacy, provide safe temporary relief. Choose a preservative-free formulation if you plan to use them more than four times a day, as preservatives can irritate the eye surface over time.

Drops treat the symptom, not the cause. If improving your screen habits and environment does not help within a week or two, see an eye doctor. Persistent dry eye can have causes unrelated to screens, including some medicines, hormonal changes and autoimmune conditions, that benefit from specific treatment.

One common mistake is reaching for eye drops marketed for red eyes, which contain vasoconstrictors to shrink blood vessels. These are for occasional use only. Used regularly, they cause rebound redness when you stop, leaving eyes redder than before.

Screen guidance for children: practical limits

A 2024 review published in Children (Basel), titled 'Media Device Use and Vision Disorders in the Pediatric Age' by Bozzola and colleagues, recommends avoiding screen exposure for children under two years except for live video calls, limiting use to one hour per day for children aged two to five, and for older children focusing on content quality and balance with physical activity and sleep rather than imposing a strict minute count.

In many Arab households, the television is on for much of the day, a smartphone is often the only available entertainment, and strict limits are hard to enforce. A realistic approach is setting clear no-screen periods, particularly before bedtime and during meals, and making outdoor play a daily priority rather than an occasional treat.

A 2022 study in The Journal of School Health by Pardhan and colleagues found that children who exceeded two hours of recreational screen time daily had significantly higher rates of visual symptoms. The key variable was recreational screen time, not educational use, though combined totals still matter.

Signs your child needs a sight test now

  • Sitting very close to the television or holding books right up to the face
  • Closing or covering one eye to see more clearly
  • Headaches or eye pain after reading or homework
  • Tripping on steps, bumping into furniture, poor hand-eye coordination
  • One eye appearing to turn in or out

When should you see an eye doctor?

Mild daily eye strain that settles overnight with rest and improves when you change your habits usually does not need a clinic visit. See an eye doctor if symptoms persist despite rest and environmental changes, if blurriness continues after you stop using a screen, if you feel actual pain in the eye rather than surface tiredness, or if you notice any change in the quality of your vision that does not correct with blinking.

If you are over 40 and finding it harder to switch focus between the screen and a paper on your desk, this is likely the beginning of presbyopia, the normal age-related loss of near focus, rather than eye strain. A corrective lens or reading glasses prescribed by an optometrist will help far more than any screen adjustment.

For adults without existing eye conditions, a full sight test every one to two years is appropriate. For those with diabetes or any condition affecting blood vessels, annual retinal checks are important regardless of screen habits. Many vision problems are entirely correctable when caught early, and there is no sensible reason to delay a check that takes less than half an hour.

Protecting your eyes in a multi-screen world

Most people today are not dealing with one screen but with several at once. A laptop open, a phone face-up beside it, and a television on across the room creates a combined visual load that is far greater than any single source. Where possible, keep one primary screen active during focus work and minimise or turn away from the others. The habit of checking the phone every few minutes during computer work prevents the visual system from ever getting a sustained break.

People who need glasses or contact lenses for distance or near vision but do not wear them at the screen are subjecting their eyes to unnecessary extra work. The eye muscles are constantly straining to compensate for an optical mismatch. Wearing the correct prescription during screen work, including reading glasses for those who need them, removes a significant portion of the daily fatigue load.

Refractive errors that go undiagnosed for years, particularly mild long-sightedness and astigmatism, are a common hidden cause of chronic headaches and eye fatigue in people who spend long hours at screens. If you have never had a formal sight test as an adult or have not had one in more than two years, that is often the quickest win available. A small prescription change can dramatically reduce daily symptoms.

Practical habits for working from home

Remote working has increased the average number of screen hours dramatically. The difference between a comfortable workday and a painful one often comes down to small setup details: chair height so your feet rest flat on the floor, screen height so your neck is straight, and enough desk depth to keep the monitor at arm's length.

Good ambient lighting matters more than most people realise. Working with a very bright screen in a near-dark room creates a harsh contrast that exhausts the visual system. Matching the room brightness to the screen brightness is one of the simplest and most effective changes you can make.

A glass of water on your desk serves double duty: it reminds you to drink, reducing the mild dehydration that worsens dry eye, and it gives you a natural reason to get up and move every hour. Physical movement during your screen breaks is better for your whole body than a break where you pick up your phone.

Frequently asked questions

Do screens permanently damage your eyes?

Current evidence does not show that screens cause permanent tissue damage in adults. The fatigue is real but reversible with rest and better habits. The more serious concern in children is myopia progression, which can be slowed with outdoor time and good habits but not fully reversed once it develops.

Do blue light glasses reduce eye strain?

The evidence for blue light glasses reducing eye strain is weak. They may reduce some discomfort for people who are sensitive to light, but they are not the core solution. Breaks, correct screen distance and good room lighting do more. Blue light filtering on your screen settings at night has a better case for protecting sleep quality than for protecting eyes.

How far should a phone be held from the face?

At least 30 centimetres. The smaller the device, the more we tend to hold it close, which increases the near-focus load on the ciliary muscles. If you find yourself holding your phone at arm's length or pushing it away to see text clearly, this may mean you need reading glasses.

How much screen time is safe for children?

Current guidance suggests avoiding screens for children under two, limiting use to one hour a day between ages two and five, and for older children focusing on balance with physical activity, sleep and face-to-face interaction rather than a strict minute count. Two hours of daily outdoor time in natural daylight is as important as any screen limit.

Can eye exercises correct myopia?

No. Eye exercises do not change the degree of existing myopia. True myopia, where the eyeball has grown too long, requires glasses, contact lenses or refractive surgery depending on the degree and age. Exercises and breaks reduce daily fatigue but cannot reshape the eye.

When should a child have their first sight test?

A first sight test is recommended between ages three and four, before starting school, even if you have not noticed a problem. Children often adapt to poor vision in ways that make it hard for parents to spot. Early detection allows treatment before it affects school performance.

Does outdoor time really protect against myopia?

Yes. Growing evidence shows that children who spend at least two hours a day outdoors in natural daylight develop myopia at lower rates and progress more slowly. Natural light levels are far higher than any indoor light source including screens, and that intensity appears to trigger a protective response in the developing eye.
